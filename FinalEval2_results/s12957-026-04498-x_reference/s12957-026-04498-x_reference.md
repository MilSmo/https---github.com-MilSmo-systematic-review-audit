Abstract Background: Conversion hepatectomy has become feasible for selected patients with initially unresectable hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), with comparative studies reporting longer survival after  surgery. We  performed  a  systematic  review,  meta-analysis,  and  quantitative  bias  analysis (QBA) to assess whether residual selection and time-dependent treatment bias could account for the reported benefit. Results: Fourteen studies contributed 24 HRs: 14 for OS and 10 for PFS/EFS. Hepatectomy was associated with lower hazards of death (HR 0.33, 95% CI 0.22-0.48) and progression or event (HR 0.41, 95% CI 0.28-0.59), although heterogeneity was substantial and both prediction intervals crossed  1.0.  E-values  suggested  robustness  to  moderate  unmeasured  confounding,  whereas extreme surgical-candidacy confounding attenuated the median bias-adjusted HR to 0.82 for OS and  1.02  for  PFS/EFS.  Immortal-time  bias  scenarios  did  not  fully  account  for  the  observed associations.  OS  small-study-effect  diagnostics  suggested  funnel-plot  asymmetry,  although sample-size-based Peters-type regression was not significant. Certainty of evidence was very low for both endpoints.

Conclusions: Conversion hepatectomy was associated with better observed survival outcomes in selected patients with initially unresectable HCC after conversion-intent therapy. The association was  robust  to  moderate,  but  not  extreme,  assumptions  about  unmeasured  surgical-candidacy confounding. These findings should be interpreted as  a  composite  post-conversion  association reflecting surgical clearance, treatment response, favorable tumor biology, and patient selection rather  than  as  the  isolated  causal  effect  of  hepatectomy.  Future  studies  should  report  surgical eligibility landmarks, time zero, time to hepatectomy, and reasons for non-resection to clarify the independent contribution of surgery within conversion pathways.

Initially  unresectable  hepatocellular  carcinoma  (HCC)  is  associated  with  poor  prognosis  and limited access to curative-intent treatment [1].  Advances in systemic therapy, including atezolizumab plus bevacizumab in IMbrave150  and tremelimumab  plus durvalumab in HIMALAYA,  have  improved  survival  in  unresectable  disease  and  expanded  the  therapeutic landscape  [2,3].  Together  with  locoregional  and  combination  strategies,  these  advances  have enabled  selected  initially  inoperable  patients  to  achieve  sufficient  tumor  regression  and  liverfunction  reserve  for  reconsideration  of  hepatectomy,  forming  the  clinical  basis  for  conversion therapy [4].

Conversion hepatectomy is increasingly considered within multidisciplinary management for selected  patients.  Cohort  studies  comparing  post-conversion  hepatectomy  with  non-surgical management generally report better survival in the surgical group. The key question is therefore not  only  whether  conversion-intent  therapy  can  achieve  tumor  response  or  resectability,  but whether proceeding to hepatectomy provides additional survival benefit once resection becomes feasible.

Previous reviews of conversion therapy in HCC have primarily focused on feasibility and antitumor activity, including objective response, conversion-to-surgery rate, resection rate, and safety  [5,6].  The  only  survival-focused  meta-analysis  was  limited  to  patients  with  complete response, a narrow setting in which conclusions varied by response definition and comparator [7]. Survival associated with proceeding to hepatectomy in the broader conversion-therapy population has not been systematically evaluated. Comparative estimates are also vulnerable to selection and timing-related  bias  because  hepatectomy  is  performed  only  after  treatment  initiation.  Surgical patients are therefore selected by survival to surgery, treatment response, liver-function reserve, and technical resectability [8]. A hazard ratio favoring hepatectomy may capture both residual tumor removal and the favorable biology and clinical profile of patients selected for surgery, while delayed exposure classification may introduce immortal-time bias if preoperative follow-up time is assigned to the surgical group [9]. Prior evidence syntheses have not formally evaluated whether residual surgical-candidacy confounding and immortal-time bias could plausibly account for the reported hepatectomy-comparison survival associations [5-7]. We conducted a systematic review and random-effects meta-analysis of comparative survival studies evaluating conversion or salvage hepatectomy in initially unresectable HCC, and applied quantitative bias analysis to assess whether residual surgical selection and surgery-related timing bias could plausibly account for the pooled associations. ## 2. Methods

## 2.1 Study design and protocol

The protocol was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (CRD420261382843). This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions and reported according to the PRISMA 2020 statement [11,14]. The literature search was updated during manuscript revision without changes to the prespecified PICO framework. No substantive amendments to the registered protocol were made. The complete database-specific search strategies are provided in Additional file 1 (Table S1).

## 2.2 Eligibility criteria

Eligible studies were full-text original clinical reports enrolling adults with initially unresectable, initially inoperable, or non-upfront-resectable hepatocellular carcinoma who received conversionintent  treatment  and  were  subsequently  compared  according  to  hepatectomy  versus  nonhepatectomy management. Eligible reports had to provide extractable comparative time-to-event HRs with 95% CIs for OS, PFS, EFS, DFS, or RFS, provided that the estimate explicitly isolated hepatectomy status.

Reports were excluded if they were limited to initially resectable neoadjuvant populations, postoperative adjuvant-only treatment, transplantation-only downstaging, ablation-only conversion, non-original articles, case reports, duplicate cohorts, prognostic-factor-only analyses, or regimen-comparison analyses that did not isolate hepatectomy status. Locoregional, hepatic arterial  infusion,  systemic,  and  multimodal  conversion-intent  regimens  were  eligible  when  the report  provided  an  extractable  comparative  HR  for  hepatectomy  versus  non-hepatectomy management. Initially unresectable disease was accepted according to the original study definition, protocol  designation,  or  multidisciplinary  treatment  context  [37].  We  did  not  retrospectively impose a uniform technical unresectability definition across heterogeneous published cohorts.

2.3 Study selection and data extraction Study  screening,  data  extraction,  estimand  classification,  and  risk-of-bias  assessment  were performed independently by two reviewers using standardized, pilot-tested extraction forms to ensure consistency across the large volume of records. Disagreements were resolved by consensus or independent third-reviewer adjudication. For reports meeting eligibility criteria, we extracted study design, treatment context, analytic cohort size, comparison definition, survival endpoint, HR, 95%  CI,  time  origin,  exposure  definition,  HR  source,  adjustment  method,  adjustment  status, covariates included in adjusted models when reported, and source location. For  each  extracted  HR,  we  recorded  whether  the  estimate  was  directly  reported,  modelderived, Kaplan-Meier-derived, adjusted, unadjusted, landmark-based, or time-dependent. HRs were  not  entered  into  synthesis  unless  the  comparison  isolated  hepatectomy  versus  nonhepatectomy management. When the original report did not provide a single unambiguous time origin, the estimate was retained if the comparison was clinically interpretable, and time origin was coded as unclear for risk-of-bias interpretation and supplementary diagnostics. HRs for age, alpha-fetoprotein, portal vein tumor thrombus, tumor burden, radiologic response, pathological response,  postoperative  recurrence  predictors,  postoperative  adjuvant  therapy,  and  treatmentregimen  comparisons  were  excluded  unless  hepatectomy  status  was  explicitly  isolated.  An extraction table was maintained for all extracted comparative survival estimates (Additional file 1:

Table S3).

## 2.4 Target contrast and HR eligibility

The  target  contrast  was  the  endpoint-specific  survival  association  comparing  patients  who underwent conversion hepatectomy with those managed without hepatectomy after conversionintent therapy. We interpreted this contrast as a composite prognostic-therapeutic association rather than as the isolated causal effect of hepatectomy. We explicitly conceptualize hepatectomy not solely as a therapeutic intervention, but also as a surrogate marker of favorable tumor biology, successful conversion, and preserved liver function.

Eligible  estimates  were  classified  according  to  the  relation  between  follow-up,  surgery assignment, and conversion eligibility. Treatment-initiation-anchored estimates began follow-up at  conversion  therapy  initiation  and  classified  exposure  by  later  receipt  of  hepatectomy. Conditional estimates began follow-up after response, conversion eligibility, radiologic complete response, or another post-treatment landmark. Time-dependent estimates modeled hepatectomy as a  time-updating  exposure. This  classification  was  used  to  describe  the  timing  structure  of  the extracted  estimates  and  to  inform  risk-of-bias  interpretation.  Target-trial  principles  were  used conceptually  to  evaluate  whether  eligibility,  treatment  assignment,  time  zero,  follow-up,  and outcome definition were aligned [12, 13]. 2.5 ROBINS-I-informed risk-of-bias assessment ARTICLE IN PRESS

Risk  of  bias  was  summarized  using  ROBINS-I-informed  domains  focused  on  confounding, participant selection, intervention classification, post-treatment timing or immortal-time concerns, endpoint  coherence,  and  overall  interpretability.  The  assessment  was  adapted  to  the  review question because hepatectomy was generally assigned after conversion-intent therapy rather than at treatment initiation.

For each endpoint-specific estimate, we recorded timing and design features relevant to bias interpretation, including clarity of time origin, timing of surgery assignment, immortal-time-bias concern, adjustment for baseline surgical candidacy, adjustment for treatment response, use of landmark  analysis,  and  use  of  time-dependent  Cox  modeling.  Endpoint-level  judgments  and support for judgment notes are provided in Additional file.

## 2.6 Certainty of evidence assessment

We assessed the certainty of the body of evidence for each synthesized outcome using an adapted GRADE framework. Certainty was judged separately for OS and PFS/EFS, considering study design,  risk  of  bias,  inconsistency,  indirectness,  imprecision,  publication  bias,  and  potential upgrading factors. ROBINS-I-informed domain judgments were used to inform the risk-of-bias domain,  while  heterogeneity  statistics,  prediction  intervals,  endpoint  definitions,  time-origin variability, and QBA findings informed the remaining domains. Because all included estimates came  from  non-randomized  comparisons  in  which  hepatectomy  was  usually  assigned  after treatment initiation, the GRADE assessment was specifically adapted to evaluate the certainty of these comparative prognostic and therapeutic associations.

## 2.7 Statistical synthesis of survival HRs

Endpoint-specific HRs were synthesized on the log-HR scale. For each HR, the standard error was calculated from the reported 95% CI as SE = [log(upper limit) - log(lower limit)] / 3.92. Separate random-effects meta-analyses were performed for OS and PFS/EFS using restricted maximum likelihood estimation. Pooled estimates were back-transformed and reported as HRs with 95% CIs. Between-study heterogeneity was summarized using I² and τ², and 95% prediction intervals were calculated to describe the expected range of effects in comparable future settings. PFS and EFS were grouped pragmatically because both capture progression- or event-related milestones prior to death; original endpoint labels were retained in study-level tables. 2.8 E-value analysis For protective associations, the inverse HR was used before applying the standard E-value formula. Confidence-limit E-values were calculated using the confidence bound closest to the null [16, 17]. ARTICLE IN PRESS

E-values  were  interpreted  as  the  minimum  strength  of  association  that  an  unmeasured confounder  would  need  to  have  with  both  receipt  of  hepatectomy  and  the  survival  outcome, conditional on measured covariates, to move the observed association to the null. This framework specifically addresses residual confounding rather than timing-related mechanisms.

## 2.9 Deterministic tipping-point QBA

Deterministic tipping-point quantitative bias analysis was performed using the endpoint-specific pooled  HRs  from  the  random-effects  syntheses  as  observed  inputs,  applying  the  standard maximum bias-factor approach for unmeasured confounding [18]. Full details of the bias-factor calculation and the clinical plausibility grid are provided in Additional file 1 (Methods S3).

## 2.10 Probabilistic QBA

Probabilistic QBA assigned prespecified log-normal distributions to the exposure-confounder and confounder-outcome  associations  under  moderate,  strong, and  extreme  surgical-candidacy confounding scenarios. Full distributional assumptions and Monte Carlo settings are reported in Additional file.

## 2.11 Aggregate-data timing-bias scenario analysis

Because individual patient-level time-to-surgery data were unavailable across studies, immortaltime  bias  could  not  be  formally  corrected  using  a  unified  time-dependent  model  [19,20].  We therefore performed an aggregate scenario analysis to examine how the pooled estimates would change under different assumptions about the time from treatment initiation to surgery and the median survival of the non-surgical  comparator group. The calculation  details are provided in Additional file.

## 2.12 Sensitivity and influence analyses

Regimen-based  subgroup  meta-analysis  was  not  planned  because  treatment  regimens  were heterogeneous and sparsely represented within comparable hepatectomy contrasts. Prespecified descriptive  diagnostics  included  leave-one-out  analysis,  exclusion  of  Kaplan-Meier-derived estimates, exclusion of extreme HRs, adjusted or time-dependent estimates only, directly reported HRs only  where  applicable,  and  influence  diagnostics.  Because  the  OS  synthesis  included  14 estimates, we assessed small-study effects and reporting-bias risk for OS using a contour-enhanced funnel  plot,  SE-based  Egger  regression,  sample-size-based  Peters-type  regression,  and  rankcorrelation testing. The PFS/EFS synthesis included 10 estimates and combined related but nonidentical endpoints; therefore, its funnel plot and regression diagnostics were treated as descriptive only.  These  analyses  were  used  to  inform  GRADE  publication-bias  judgments  rather  than  to redefine the main synthesis. ARTICLE IN PRESS

## 2.13 Software and reproducibility

Analyses were performed in R version 4.5.3 using prespecified analysis code. Random-effects meta-analysis, prediction intervals, leave-one-out analysis, influence diagnostics, and small-studyeffect tests were implemented with the metafor package [15]. E-value calculations, deterministic tipping-point QBA, probabilistic QBA, timing-bias scenario analyses, and funnel-plot diagnostics were applied to  the  extracted  comparative  HR  dataset.  Package  versions,  session  information, extraction tables, and analysis code were archived to support reproducibility [21].

## 3. Results

## 3.1 Study selection and included studies

Fourteen comparative survival studies met the eligibility  criteria and contributed 24 endpointspecific  HRs  comparing  hepatectomy  with  non-hepatectomy  management,  including  14  OS estimates and 10 PFS/EFS estimates (Figure 1).

## 3.2 Study and estimand characteristics

The 14 studies included analytic cohorts ranging from 36 to 1262 patients. Treatment contexts included tyrosine kinase inhibitor- and immune checkpoint inhibitor-based combinations, HAICbased  strategies,  TACE/HAIC-containing  multimodal  therapy,  and  real-world  immune-based conversion regimens. Comparator definitions generally contrasted patients undergoing conversion or  salvage  hepatectomy  with  patients  managed  without  hepatectomy  after  conversion-intent therapy. Time origin varied across studies. Some estimates were anchored at treatment initiation, some were conditional on response or conversion eligibility, and one study used a time-dependent Cox  framework.  Study  characteristics  are  summarized  in  Table  1,  with  full  study  details  in Additional file 1: Table S2. Endpoint-specific HRs with corresponding E-values are listed in Table 2, and the complete extraction table is provided in Additional file 1: Table S3.

Ten PFS/EFS-specific HRs were included. Conversion hepatectomy was associated with a lower hazard of progression or qualifying event than non-surgical management (HR 0.41, 95% CI 0.280.59). Heterogeneity was substantial (I² = 64.3%; τ² = 0.197), and the 95% prediction interval included the null value (0.16-1.05) (Figure 3 and Table 3).

## 3.5 Robustness to unmeasured surgical-candidacy confounding

The pooled OS estimate had a point-estimate E-value of 5.54 and a confidence-limit E-value of 3.58. The corresponding values for PFS/EFS were 4.34 and 2.81, respectively (Figure 4).

## 3.6 Bias thresholds under deterministic QBA

In deterministic tipping-point QBA, modest combinations of surgical-candidacy imbalance and confounder-outcome association did not move the pooled HRs to the null. Stronger joint bias assumptions moved the bias-adjusted HRs toward the null, particularly for PFS/EFS. The rule-out contour identified the combinations at which the adjusted HR reached 1 (Figure 5).

## 3.7 Probabilistic bias-adjusted survival associations

Probabilistic QBA shifted the simulated bias-adjusted HR distributions toward the null. For OS, median bias-adjusted HRs were 0.42, 0.55, and 0.82 under moderate, strong, and extreme surgicalcandidacy confounding scenarios, respectively; the corresponding probabilities that the adjusted HR remained below 1.0 were 1.000, 0.978, and 0.713. For PFS/EFS, median bias-adjusted HRs were 0.52, 0.68, and 1.02, with corresponding probabilities of 0.995, 0.901, and 0.478 (Figures S1 and S2).

## 3.8 Sensitivity to surgery-timing assumptions

Aggregate timing-bias scenario analysis used the pooled HRs as observed inputs (OS HRobs = 0.33; PFS/EFS HRobs = 0.41). Longer assumed intervals from treatment initiation to surgery and shorter  assumed  control  survival  attenuated  the  pooled  estimates  toward  the  null. Across  the prespecified grid, the scenario-adjusted estimates remained below 1.0 for both OS and PFS/EFS (Additional file 1: Figure S3 and Table S9).

## 3.10 Small-study effects and reporting-bias assessment

3.9 Bias profile of the comparative evidence ROBINS-I-informed  risk-of-bias  assessment  showed  that  the  comparative  evidence  remained vulnerable to residual confounding, participant selection, and surgery timing during follow-up. Although  the  pooled  estimates  were  below  1.0,  individual  estimates  varied  in  direction  and precision,  and  the  ROBINS-I-informed  assessment  indicated  serious  residual  confounding  and timing concerns (Figure 6; endpoint-level judgments are provided in Additional file 1: Figure S4 and Tables S4-S5). For  OS  (k  =  14),  the  contour-enhanced  funnel  plot  showed  asymmetry,  with  several  smaller estimates falling in statistically significant protective contours. SE-based Egger regression (t = - 3.71, p = 0.003) and rank-correlation testing (Kendall's tau = -0.47, p = 0.019) supported funnelplot asymmetry, whereas the sample-size-based Peters-type regression was not significant (t = 0.77, p = 0.456). This pattern suggests possible small-study effects, selective reporting, or true betweenstudy heterogeneity, but it does not prove publication bias. For PFS/EFS (k = 10), the funnel plot and regression diagnostics were treated as descriptive because the synthesis was at the minimum threshold and combined related but non-identical endpoints ( Figure S5 and Table S15).

## 3.11 Descriptive sensitivity diagnostics

Descriptive supplementary diagnostics described the influence of individual estimates and selected analytic assumptions. These analyses were interpreted descriptively and did not change the overall direction of the pooled estimates (Additional file 1: Tables S10A-S10B). In diagnostics restricted to adjusted or time-dependent estimates, heterogeneity was lower and pooled estimates remained below 1.0 (OS HR 0.21 [95% CI 0.13-0.34]; PFS/EFS HR 0.35 [95% CI 0.24-0.49]).

## 3.12 Certainty of evidence

The certainty of evidence was rated as very low for both OS and PFS/EFS. For OS, the pooled association favored hepatectomy, but certainty was downgraded. For PFS/EFS, certainty was also rated as very low. Detailed domain-by-domain judgments are provided in Table S12.

## 4. Discussion

In  this  systematic  review  and  meta-analysis,  conversion  hepatectomy  was  associated  with improved OS and PFS/EFS compared with non-surgical management in selected patients with initially  unresectable  HCC.  However,  substantial  between-study  heterogeneity  and  prediction intervals crossing the null indicate that the magnitude of benefit is unlikely to be uniform across clinical  settings.  These  findings  support  the  potential  value  of  hepatectomy  after  successful conversion therapy, while emphasizing the need to interpret the observed survival advantage in the context of patient selection and time-dependent treatment assignment. Prior  reviews  of  conversion  therapy  in  HCC  have  mainly  addressed  whether  initially unresectable tumors can be downstaged to resection, focusing on tumor response, conversion-tosurgery rate, resection rate, safety, and perioperative outcomes [5,6]. Less attention has been given to  the  subsequent  clinical  decision  faced  after  successful  downstaging:  whether  proceeding  to hepatectomy adds survival benefit beyond the prognosis already conferred by treatment response, preserved  liver  function,  and  favorable  tumor  biology.  The  present  review  addresses  this distinction  by  focusing  on  comparative  survival  after  conversion  or  salvage  hepatectomy. The pooled  associations  favored  hepatectomy  for  both  OS  and  PFS/EFS,  and  E-value  analyses suggested that moderate unmeasured confounding alone would be unlikely to fully explain these associations. However, the magnitude of benefit should not be interpreted as the isolated causal effect of surgery. Patients selected for hepatectomy are those who survive to surgical assessment, respond sufficiently to therapy, maintain adequate liver reserve, and remain technically resectable. Accordingly, the observed survival advantage is best interpreted as the outcome of a successful conversion  pathway,  in  which  surgical  clearance  may  contribute  to  the  observed  survival association within a highly selected responder population. This interpretation is directly relevant to  multidisciplinary  decision-making:  hepatectomy should be  considered for  carefully selected patients  after  effective  downstaging,  while  the  strength  of  the  survival  advantage  remains dependent on selection criteria, timing of surgical assessment, and the completeness of bias control in the underlying studies.

The observed survival advantage should be interpreted within the clinical context of postconversion patient selection. Hepatectomy may improve outcomes by removing residual viable tumor after effective systemic or multimodal therapy, and pathological response after conversion surgery has been associated with recurrence-free and overall survival [34]. However, radiologic response,  alpha-fetoprotein  decline,  radiologic  complete  response,  and  pathological  complete response  represent  different  dimensions  of  treatment  response  and  should  not  be  treated  as equivalent surrogates for tumor clearance [28,35]. In clinical practice, patients who proceed to hepatectomy  are  usually  those  with  sustained  disease  control,  adequate  liver-function  reserve, preserved performance status, controlled or absent extrahepatic disease, and technically resectable lesions  [36].  These  factors  are  themselves  prognostic  and  may  partly  explain  the  survival differences between surgical and non-surgical groups. Interpretation is further complicated by the heterogeneity  of  conversion-intent  regimens,  including  TACE,  HAIC,  immune  checkpoint inhibitors, tyrosine kinase inhibitors, and their combinations. These strategies differ in antitumor activity, depth of response, liver toxicity, and probability of conversion, thereby influencing both eligibility for surgery and subsequent prognosis. Accordingly, the pooled HRs are best understood as reflecting survival within a selected post-conversion surgical pathway rather than a pure surgical treatment effect. ARTICLE IN PRESS

The QBA findings help define the evidential boundaries of this interpretation. These analyses were used not to isolate the causal effect of hepatectomy, but to assess how robust the observed survival  associations  were  to  plausible  residual  bias.  Moderate  unmeasured  confounding  was insufficient to fully attenuate the pooled associations, whereas stronger assumptions about surgical candidacy reduced the apparent benefit, particularly for PFS/EFS. Similarly, because hepatectomy was assigned after conversion-intent therapy had begun in several studies, discordance between the start of follow-up and the definition of surgical exposure could introduce immortal-time bias if preoperative event-free time was attributed to the hepatectomy group. The timing-bias scenario analysis  should  therefore  be  interpreted  as  a  study-level  sensitivity  analysis  rather  than  an individual-patient correction. Taken together, the QBA results support the presence of a clinically meaningful survival signal after conversion hepatectomy, but they also indicate that this signal likely  reflects  the  combined  effects  of  effective  downstaging,  surgical  tumor  clearance,  and selection of patients with favorable biology and preserved clinical reserve, rather than the isolated effect of surgery alone.

Several  limitations  should  be  considered. All  included  studies  were  non-randomized  and varied  in  unresectability  criteria,  conversion  regimens,  endpoint  definitions,  and  adjustment methods.  As  a  result,  the  pooled  HRs  should  be  interpreted  as  average  associations  across heterogeneous clinical settings rather than as a uniform treatment effect. Some estimates were unadjusted  or  reconstructed  from  Kaplan-Meier  curves,  and  the  number  and  heterogeneity  of studies still limited regimen  subgroup  analyses and made  small-study-effect diagnostics exploratory, especially for PFS/EFS. The OS funnel plot suggested asymmetry, which could reflect publication  bias,  selective  reporting,  true  heterogeneity,  or  small-study  effects;  however,  the Peters-type sample-size regression was not significant. Although QBA helped assess the potential impact  of  residual  confounding  and  immortal-time  bias,  it  could  not  replace  individual-level information  on  surgical  eligibility,  time  to  hepatectomy,  and  reasons  for  non-resection.  These constraints  explain  the  very  low  certainty  of  evidence  and  highlight  the  need  for  prospective cohorts,  individual-patient-data  analyses,  registry-based  target-trial  emulation,  or  randomized comparisons where equipoise exists. 5. Conclusion ARTICLE IN PRESS

Conversion hepatectomy was associated with improved survival in selected patients with initially unresectable HCC after conversion-intent therapy. This association appeared robust to moderate residual confounding, but its magnitude may be influenced by patient selection, timing bias, and small-study effects. Future studies should define surgical eligibility landmarks, time zero, time to hepatectomy,  and  reasons  for  non-resection  to  better  distinguish  treatment  effect  from  patient selection.
