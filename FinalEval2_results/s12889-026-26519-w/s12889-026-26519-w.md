## Point-of-care testing for non-communicable diseases and health literacy outcomes: a systematic review

<!-- image -->

Kelcie Miller 1* , Lauren Duckworth 1 , Tamika Regnier 1 , Susan Matthews 1 , Igor Toskin 2 and Mark Shephard 1

## Abstract

Background Health literacy responsiveness has been identified as a key action area in reducing the burden of noncommunicable diseases (NCDs), which are a leading cause of death worldwide. Point-of-care (POC) testing has the potential to improve health literacy in individuals and communities. This systematic review aimed to synthesise the literature regarding POC testing for NCDs and health literacy outcomes.

Methods A systematic search was conducted across seven databases, Medline (via OVID), PsycINFO, Emcare, Scopus, ProQuest (Health databases), CINAHL, and Web of Science, between May 2018 and May 2023. Studies that investigated the utilisation of a POC device for measurement of NCD markers, and reported at least one outcome measure of health literacy in individuals over 10 years old were eligible for inclusion. Risk of bias in included studies was assessed using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT). Results were mapped to the five WHO health literacy actions through narrative synthesis.

Results Thirteen studies were eligible for inclusion, primarily investigating POC assessment of diabetes, with some examining lipids or coagulation. Study cohorts were geographically limited to eight countries in predominantly high-income settings. Following POC testing relevant to diabetes and cardiovascular contexts, patients reported an enhanced understanding of health, motivation to make positive lifestyle changes, increased quality of life and decreased worry, increased confidence, and improved health markers.

Conclusion The findings from this review suggest that POC testing may support improvements in individual health literacy outcomes relevant to NCD management, specifically in diabetes and cardiovascular contexts. These findings reflect the potential of POC testing to contribute to more responsive health systems, by improving accessibility, results delivery, and communication of health information. However, the evidence base is limited in scope, and findings may not necessarily be generalisable. Future studies should prioritise examining a broader range of NCDs, particularly in underdeveloped regions, and employ specific validated health literacy tools.

## Background

Non-communicable diseases (NCDs) are a major global health  challenge,  accounting  for  more  than  a  third  of the disease burden and 74% of all deaths [1, 2]. Of these deaths, more than three-quarters occur in low- to middle-income countries (LMICs) [2]. NCDs such as cardiovascular  disease,  diabetes,  chronic  respiratory  diseases, cancer,  and  mental  health  conditions  impose  major health and economic burdens on individuals, communities, healthcare systems, and governments, and are therefore key targets for public health intervention [1]. NCD development is highly influenced by modifiable risk factors  such  as  smoking,  alcohol  consumption,  physical inactivity,  diet  quality,  air  pollution,  and  environmental stressors  [1].  As  such,  interventions  to  reduce  the  burden of NCDs often focus on prevention via behavioural change [1].

Given  the  scale  of  the  NCD  burden,  timely  diagnosis  and  effective  monitoring  are  critical.  Point-of-care (POC) testing provides an accessible method of detecting and managing NCDs through the delivery of immediate results to inform clinical decision making [3]. POC testing is defined as diagnostic testing performed at or near the site of care, with results available during the patient encounter, and without the need for specimens to be sent to a central laboratory [3]. Results may then be communicated directly to patients, or via healthcare providers. For NCDs, POC testing options are available for monitoring  cardiovascular  disease  markers  (e.g.  lipid  profiles,  international  normalised  ratio;  INR),  diabetes  (e.g. HbA1c, blood glucose), and chronic respiratory diseases (e.g.  exhaled  nitric  oxide;  FeNO).  Mental  health  conditions are not currently assessed via pathology-based POC diagnostics,  and  although  there  are  emerging  applications  for  cancer  detection  and  monitoring  (e.g.  tumour markers), POC tests are not yet routinely implemented.

A fundamental advantage of POC testing is its capacity to bridge the gap in health equity by improving access and engagement [4-7]. Not only does POC testing benefit health access both geographically and culturally, it can also  enhance  patient  engagement,  through  both  the  act of testing and the communication of results [7]. Importantly,  how  results  are  communicated,  whether  directly to  patients  or  via  healthcare  providers,  may  influence how individuals understand and implement their health information.

Health  literacy  refers  to  the  knowledge,  confidence, and  comfort  of  individuals  and  communities  in  accessing,  understanding,  appraising,  remembering  and  using health-based information [8]. Earlier definitions focused on an individual's literacy and numeracy skills in healthcare  contexts,  but  the  concept  has  evolved  to  include both  individual  capacities  and  the  organisational  structures  that  support  them  [1,  9].  Poor  health  literacy  has been  associated  with  increased  hospitalisations,  lower use  of  preventive  measures  such  as  vaccines,  improper medication use, low health status,  and  higher  mortality rates [10]. As an important determinant of health, poor health literacy can exacerbate existing health inequalities influenced by socioeconomic and cultural factors [11].

The World Health Organization (WHO) highlights five actions  that  demonstrate  health  literacy: Access , Understand , Appraise , Remember ,  and Use [8].  Individuals with  adequate  health  literacy  not  only  have  the  ability to access , understand ,  and appraise health  information, but  also remember to  implement  and use healthy  skills and  behaviours,  such  as  taking  medications  or  attending  appointments  [8].  These  action  domains  provide the framework for this review. Beyond direct health literacy, related constructs such as activation, engagement, and  empowerment  also  shape  how  individuals  interact with health information. Patient activation refers to the knowledge, skills, and confidence in managing one's own health  [12];  engagement  reflects  active  involvement  in healthcare  processes  [13];  and  empowerment  refers  to gaining  control  over  health  decisions  and  actions  [14]. Although  these  constructs  are  not  direct  measures  of health  literacy,  they  capture  dimensions  of  confidence, knowledge, and motivation that overlap with the WHO health literacy actions.

While  health  literacy  refers  to  individual  capacities, health  literacy  responsiveness  describes  the  ability  of health systems  and  organisations  to  create  environments that support engagement with health information and  services.  Health  literacy  responsiveness  has  been identified  as  a  key  action  area  in  reducing  the  burden of  NCDs [15]. The development and implementation of interventions  and  technologies  that  enhance  health  literacy responsiveness are therefore of great public health interest  [16].  With  its  immediate  patient  involvement, POC testing  has  the  potential  to  strengthen  health  literacy responsiveness by improving accessibility, communication,  and  patient-centred  workflows  [3].  Enhanced responsiveness can, in turn, support individual health literacy outcomes by improving understanding, confidence, and engagement with health information.

This  systematic  review  therefore  aimed  to  synthesise the current literature regarding POC testing and health literacy  outcomes  in  relation  to  NCDs,  including  cardiovascular  diseases,  chronic  respiratory  diseases,  and diabetes,  in  the  context  of  the  WHO  health  literacy framework.

## Methods

## Protocol and registration

The review protocol was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; registration  number  CRD42022348011).  A  full  protocol may  be  requested  from  the  authors.  Amendments  to the original protocol included changes in author contributions,  and  health  literacy  outcomes  were  synthesised according  to  the  World  Health  Organization  (WHO) health literacy domains. The review was conducted and reported  in  line  with  the  Preferred  Reporting  Items  for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement (Additional file 1) [17].

## Selection criteria

Records were eligible for inclusion if they met the following criteria:

- (i)	the use of at least one POC testing device assessing NCD measures, including cardiovascular disease (e.g. lipid profiles, coagulation markers), chronic respiratory disease (e.g. FeNO), or diabetes (e.g. HbA1c, blood glucose). POC testing was considered across multiple contexts, including adult care, paediatric care, self-managed testing, clinic-based testing, or community health settings;
- (ii)	 reporting of at least one measure related to health literacy or health literacy responsiveness, including outcomes mapped to the five WHO health literacy actions ( Access , Understand , Appraise , Remember , Use ). This could include direct assessment of health literacy using validated instruments, or proxy measures such as patient activation, knowledge, awareness, confidence, satisfaction, engagement, behaviour change, or relevant clinical indicators;
- (iii) health literacy of individuals aged 10 + years, including older children and adolescents, with caregivers able to report their own health literacy outcomes based on POC assessment of a child;
- (iv) full-text peer reviewed journal article published within the five years prior to the search.

Studies  were  excluded  if  they  were  reviews  of  peerreviewed studies.

The five-year limit was applied in line with WHO guidance  to  capture  contemporary  evidence  on  POC  testing and health literacy, reflecting advances in diagnostic technologies  and  evolving  definitions  of  health  literacy. No restrictions on the type of study design, sample size, or language of manuscript were imposed.

## Information sources

Records  were  identified  by  a  research  librarian  and researcher  (KM)  through  searches  of  electronic  databases from 5 May 2018 to the date of the search, 5 May 2023. Databases searched included Medline (via OVID), PsycINFO (via OVID), Emcare (via OVID), Scopus, ProQuest  (Health  and  Medicine  databases  only),  CINAHL

(via EBSCO), and Web of Science (via Clarivate). Citation searches were not conducted.

## Search strategy

The search strategy was developed in collaboration with an  experienced  research  librarian.  The  search  strategy consisted  of  MeSH  terms  including  Point-of-Care  Testing;  Health  Promotion;  Health  Knowledge,  Attitudes, Practice;  Patient  Education  as  Topic;  Health  Literacy; Health Education; Life Style; Diet; Exercise; Health Behaviour; Smoking Cessation; Smoking Reduction; Tobacco Use Cessation; Alcohol Abstinence; Social Behaviour; and Social Participation.  Keywords included domains  of  point-of-care  (point  of  care,  bedside,  rapid, handheld, remote, mobile), testing (test, screening, diagnosis,  monitor),  patient  (health,  informed  consent,  disease,  condition),  knowledge  (awareness,  understanding, competence,  comprehension),  and  behaviour  (lifestyle, weight,  diet,  physical  activity,  alcohol,  social,  engage, community, smoking, tobacco).

The term 'review' was excluded by title and manuscript type.  No  filters  were  applied.  A  full  search  strategy  by database can be found in Additional file 2.

## Selection process

Resulting  records  from  each  database  were  imported into  EndNote  20  where  duplicates  were  provisionally removed.  Records  were  then  uploaded  to  Covidence systematic  review  management  software  [18]  where  a final  duplication  detection  test  was  conducted.  Titles and abstracts of resulting records were initially screened against  eligibility  criteria  by  two  researchers  (KM  and LD), independently. Agreement between reviewers was  high  at  96.7%,  with  consensus  required  for  further screening.  Remaining  records  underwent  independent full-text screening by the same two researchers (KM and LD), with consensus required for inclusion in the review. Agreement  at  full-text  screening  was  85.7%,  with  discrepancies  resolved  through  discussion  or,  if  necessary, adjudication by a third reviewer (MS).

## Data collection

Results from eligible studies, including descriptive data, were  extracted  using  a  study-specific  form  created  in Covidence  [18].  Data  extraction  was  completed  independently by the same two researchers that screened the records,  with  discrepancies  resolved  by  communication between  the  two  researchers.  Resulting  data  were  then exported to Excel.

## Data items

Data  extracted  from  each  included  study  consisted  of: authors, publication year, country, dates study was undertaken, cohort description, participant demographics

(e.g.  age,  sex/gender,  race),  sample  size,  study  design, data  collection  methods,  exposure  type  (i.e.  POC  tests and  measures),  data  analyses,  and  outcome  data  (i.e. health literacy measures). Health literacy measures could include direct assessment of health literacy, or indicators of health literacy via proxy measures such as patient activation, knowledge, motivation, adherence and behaviour change (validated or self-reported).

## Risk of bias in individual studies

Risk  of  bias  (quality)  of  individual  studies  was  assessed using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT) [19]. The MMAT assessed included studies based on the study design;  qualitative  only,  quantitative  (non-randomised, randomised,  descriptive),  or  mixed  methods.  Risk  of bias was assessed by two independent researchers, with any discrepancies resolved through discussion or a third reviewer.  Studies  were  not  allocated  a  rating  based  on recommendations from the MMAT author [19].

## Data synthesis

Meta-analysis  was  not  feasible  due  to  heterogeneity  in intervention  type  (e.g.  community  screening  vs.  clinical monitoring),  disease  focus  (e.g.  diabetes,  cardiovascular), outcome measurement tools (e.g. Patient Activation Measure,  PAM-13;  behaviour  change),  and  population characteristics, which restricted comparability. We therefore  employed  a  framework-guided  narrative  synthesis. Qualitative and quantitative outcome data were extracted and deductively coded against the five WHO health literacy actions ( Access , Understand , Appraise , Remember , Use )  to  enable thematic comparison. Each outcome was mapped to the domains that best reflected the construct measured, and findings were then categorised by condition and type of POC test.

## Results

## Study selection

The  database  searches  resulted  in  the  identification  of 12,459 records (Fig. 1). After removing duplicates, 6,129 unique records were retained for screening (Fig. 1). Title and abstract screening resulted in 98 records for further screening.  Of  these,  11  reports  could  not  be  retrieved (abstract-only, n =  7; non-written format, n =  2; inaccessible, n =  2), leaving 87 articles retained for full-text screening  (Fig.  1).  Thirteen  articles  were  eligible  for  inclusion following  the  final  screening  stage  (Fig.  1).  This  reduction highlights the limited availability of recent evidence investigating POC testing and health literacy outcomes.

## Study characteristics

Thirteen studies were included in the review, with a total sample size of 21,265. Studies assessed POC testing exposures  for  management  of  diabetes  [20-30],  lipids  [20,

30]  and/or  coagulation  [29,  31,  32]  (Table  1).  Although chronic respiratory diseases were included in the search scope,  no  eligible  studies  assessing  health  literacy  outcomes  were  identified.  Across  conditions,  POC  testing was  implemented  in  varying  contexts,  including  adult outpatient care [23, 24], paediatric diabetes management (with parental health literacy outcomes) [21, 22], at-home self-testing  (e.g.  INR  testing)  [31,  32],  and  communitybased screening [27, 30].

Health  literacy  outcomes  were  reported  through  a range  of  proxy  measures,  including  patient  activation [25, 28], patient knowledge and experiences [21, 31, 32], Likert scale ratings of understanding [29, 30], qualitative interviews  [20-24,  27],  medication  adherence  [26,  31, 32], and behaviour change [21, 30]. As detailed in Table 2,  measurement tools varied widely, including validated instruments  such  as  PAM-13,  clinical  indicators  (i.e. HbA1c, INR, and PDC), and study-developed questionnaires,  knowledge  scores,  Likert  scales,  and  qualitative interviews.

Included  studies  were  conducted  in  eight  countries: England, India, Ireland, Italy, New Zealand, South Korea, and the United States. The mean ages of adults assessed ranged  from  31  to  70  years,  with  two  studies  assessing parental outcomes of children with type 1 diabetes [21, 22].  Basic  demographic  characteristics  such  as  age  and sex were consistently reported, however reporting of ethnicity  was varied, with most studies not reporting data. Where reported, participants were predominantly European or non-Hispanic White [21-23, 28, 30], with limited representation of minority groups such as Asian or African  ethnicity  groups  [23,  30].  One  study  included  only Black men [27]. Other equity considerations, particularly socioeconomic status, were rarely reported, limiting synthesis  across  population  subgroups.  A  summary  of  the included studies can be seen in Table 1.

## Risk of bias within studies

Eight  studies  met  al.l  quality  criteria  assessed  by  the MMAT  [20-26,  28].  The  MMAT  assessment  showed consistently  clear  research  questions  and  appropriate data collection across most studies. However, two quantitative studies did not meet criteria for the interpretation of results based on the data provided [27, 29], while two did not account for confounders in analyses [31, 32]. One descriptive study failed to meet the criteria of low nonresponse bias [30]. All results from the risk of bias assessment can be seen in Additional file 3.

## Access

Two  qualitative  studies  reported  participants  felt  more confident  having  assessment  at  the  POC,  as  they  were able to have an immediate discussion with the clinician after  receiving  results,  which  allowed  them  to  receive advice and ask questions as they arose [20, 23]. Parents of children with type 1 diabetes reported that continuous access  to  results  supported  their  confidence  in  managing their child's condition [21, 22]. These outcomes were self-reported and  reflect  perceived  improvements  in access rather than independently verified changes.

## Understand

Several qualitative studies of diabetes patients found that participants  reported  a  greater  understanding  of  their own [23, 24] or their child's [21, 22] physical health following POC testing interventions, supporting a reduction in adverse events. Participants described improved comprehension  of  test  results  and  clinical  thresholds  [20, 23].  Multiple  studies  also  observed  that  this  improved understanding extended to lifestyle behaviours in diabetes  patients,  such  as  diet,  and  the  mechanisms  through which they contributed to their physical health outcomes [22-24].

Considering  quantitative  results,  Watson  and  Rajbhandari,  et  al.  [30]  reported  that  attendance  at  community Health  Melas (Hindi  term  for  'fair'),  where  an overall  health  check  was  conducted,  including  random glucose and cholesterol POC testing, 98.4% of attendees reported an increased understanding of their health, and 93.1% were encouraged to make lifestyle changes [30]. As these  events  involved  multiple  health  promotion  activities  in  addition  to  POC  testing,  potential  confounding must be considered when interpreting findings. A further two studies examined the change in HbA1c and PAM-13 scores  following  POC  testing  intervention,  with  PAM13  scores  increasing  on  average  by  5.4  to  5.7  [25,  28]. Another study of patients on Warfarin found an average of a 29% increase in self-reported knowledge scores following  POC  monitoring  [32].  While  PAM-13  is  a  validated instrument, other measures were author-developed or  self-reported  proxies,  limiting  comparability  across studies.

## Appraise

Improved quality of life and reduced worry was reported in four qualitative studies [21, 22, 24, 29], with two based on parental feedback [21, 22]. Other interviews described increased satisfaction, empowerment,  and confidence in managing health [23, 24, 27]. Among thrombosis centre  patients  on  long-term  vitamin  K  antagonist  (VKA) therapy,  92.0%  of  those  adopting  an  at-home  self-testing intervention were satisfied with monitoring, compared to 39.0% under standard processes [31]. Intervention patients also reported greater comfort with side effects, and 78.0% indicated perceived improvements in quality of life [31].

## Remember

In qualitative interviews, participants reported that POC testing  encouraged  them  to  recall  and  clarify  health information during consultations [20, 23] and was associated  with  more  consistent  medication  adherence  [23] in  diabetes  contexts.  These  insights  were  supported  by quantitative measures. Munshi and Amelung, et al. [26] reported that patients had a higher likelihood of remaining adherent to antidiabetic drugs compared to controls (β = 1.4, 95% CI = 1.3, 1.5), with adherence reflected in increased  proportion  of  days  covered  (PDC)  [26].  Barcellona and Mastino, et al. [31] found that patients using at-home INR testing achieved higher time in therapeutic range  (TTR),  ranging  from  40.0%  to  100.0%  (median  = 77.0%),  compared  to  24.0%  to  89.0%  (median  =  71.0%) in  controls.  Similarly,  another  study  reported  improved INR  control,  with  the  proportion  of  patients  in-range increasing  from  77.5%  pre-intervention  to  87.3%  postintervention [32].

## Use

Participants  self-reported  adoption  of  positive  lifestyle behaviours, such as dietary changes and increased physical  activity  [21-24,  30].  At  community Health  Melas (fairs)  73.6%  of  attendees  who  had  previously  undergone a health check self-reported lifestyle changes afterwards [30]. Clinical outcomes showed reductions in high HbA1c levels (&gt; 9%) from approximately 21% pre-intervention, to approximately 10% post-intervention [25, 28]. As  described  above,  studies  also  reported  associations with  improved  INR  control  [31,  32]  and  greater  adherence to antidiabetic drugs [26]. While clinical measures support some findings, particularly in regards to medication  adherence, lifestyle  changes  were  self-reported  and should be interpreted with caution.

## Discussion

To  our  knowledge,  this  study  is  among  the  first  to  systematically review the literature  on  pathology-based POC testing  for  NCDs  focusing  on  health  literacy  outcomes.  Following  systematic  searches,  thirteen  studies  conducted  across  eight  countries  were  identified, investigating POC testing for diabetes/glucose, lipids, or coagulation management. POC testing is not yet widely available  for  the  direct  assessment  of  cancer  or  mental health conditions, and no eligible studies were identified for  chronic  respiratory  diseases.  As  such,  the  evidence base is restricted to diabetes and cardiovascular contexts, with most studies focused on diabetes. Included studies employed  diverse  methodologies  (quantitative,  qualitative,  and  mixed  methods),  but  findings  were  generally consistent despite heterogeneity. Evidence suggested that access to POC testing may be associated with improvements across the WHO's five actions of health literacy: Access , Understand , Appraise , Remember , and Use health information.

Qualitative interviews consistently identified that access to  health  information was perceived as enhanced through  the  immediacy  of  POC  test  results  [20-24]. Rapid feedback of results was reported to reduce delays and  uncertainty,  creating  opportunities  for  patients  to engage and communicate with clinicians more effectively [20, 24]. This was a consistent theme for parents of children  with  diabetes,  who  reported  increased  confidence in managing their child's condition [21, 22]. These findings suggest that the rapid nature of POC testing may be associated  with  supporting  health  literacy,  not  only  by improving direct access to health information, but also by fostering more meaningful interactions between patients and health professionals.

Patients  and  parents  using  POC  technologies  for  diabetes  monitoring  also  reported  enhanced understanding of  their  own  [23,  24]  or  their  child's  [21,  22]  health, including  greater  awareness  of  how  lifestyle  habits  (e.g. diet)  influenced  outcomes  [22-24],  and  improved  comprehension  of  test  results  and  clinical  thresholds  [20, 23].  These  insights  were  complemented  by  quantitative  proxy  measures,  including  improvements  in  PAM13  scores  [25,  28],  self-rated  knowledge  scores  [32], and Likert scale  assessments  of  understanding  [30]  and empowerment [29]. While PAM-13 is not a direct measure of health literacy, it is a validated tool that assesses an individual's knowledge, skills, and confidence in managing  their  health  [12],  aligning  with  WHO's  actions  of appraising , understanding , and using health information. Together,  these  findings  suggest  that  POC  testing  may be associated with improved clinical understanding and reinforcement of the connection between health and lifestyle behaviours.

Appraising health  information  involves  evaluating  its relevance  and  implications  for  management  [8],  which was reflected through reports of reduced uncertainty and increased  confidence  in  managing  health.  In  qualitative studies, participants reported that POC testing for diabetes and coagulation reduced worry and improved quality of  life  [21,  22,  24,  29],  while  others  described  increased satisfaction, empowerment, and confidence in managing their  health  [23,  24,  27].  These  findings  were  supported by quantitative improvements in PAM-13 scores [25, 28], and higher satisfaction and quality of life ratings [31].

In  qualitative  interviews,  diabetes  patients  reported remembering and  clarifying  health  information  at  the time of consultation when POC testing was used [20, 23]. POC testing in diabetes contexts was also associated with more consistent medication adherence [23]. These observations were reinforced by findings in coagulation monitoring, where associations with improved adherence was reflected in increased proportion of days covered (PDC) [26] and INR control [31, 32]. These results suggest that POC  testing  interventions  may  assist  recall  of  health requirements, supported by measurable associations with improvements in treatment adherence.

The ability to access , understand , appraise , and remember health information appeared to be associated with the translation of knowledge into action through use. Across studies, participants reported adoption of positive lifestyle behaviours, such as dietary changes and increased physical activity [21-24, 30]. Quantitative data showed associations with improvements in medication adherence [26, 31, 32], and reductions in high HbA1c levels (&gt; 9%) [25, 28]. Even a 1% decrease in HbA1c is associated with a significant reduction in diabetes-related complications, including cardiovascular disease, kidney damage, nerve damage, and  eye  problems  [33,  34].  Collectively,  these  findings indicate that POC testing interventions may facilitate the translation of health information into action, supporting lifestyle change and improved disease management.

It is important to note that POC testing is rarely delivered  in  isolation,  with  communication  of  results  also strongly  influencing  patient  understanding  and  engagement. Mechanisms such as the immediacy of results, personalised provider explanation, and access to additional resources may  reinforce knowledge,  confidence,  and comfort. Visible biomarker changes may strengthen the perceived link between behaviour and health outcomes, while  integration  into  routine  care  can  help  normalise engagement and build confidence. In practice, POC testing was commonly delivered alongside broader care such as  health  consultations,  community  health  events,  and additional  health  measurements  (e.g.  blood  pressure). These multi-layered interventions introduce several factors that may shape health knowledge and behaviour, and therefore,  the  observed  outcomes  cannot  be  attributed to POC testing alone. Rather, the findings highlight how POC testing, when combined with responsive communication and supportive care processes, may contribute to health literacy outcomes.

## Strengths and limitations

This  study  was  strengthened  by  the  rigorous  systematic review process conducted in line with the PRISMA statement [17]. Eligibility criteria were explicitly defined prior to commencing the literature search, with no restrictions imposed on language, region, or study type. The search strategy was methodologically developed in collaboration with  an  experienced  research  librarian,  following  multiple pilot searches to refine database queries and MeSH terms  across  several  databases  to  maximise  coverage. Although comprehensive, the omission of device-specific and  risk-factor  terms  may  have  limited  retrieval  of  eligible studies, and citation searching was not undertaken. Future  reviews  should  expand  search  terms  to  include specific NCDs, risk factors, and device-specific terminology to ensure broader coverage.

Study  screening  and  data  extraction  were  independently  undertaken  by  two  researchers,  achieving  high agreement, which improves reliability compared to single screening methods [35]. Included studies were limited to publications  from  the  five  years  prior  to  the  search,  an intentional  scope  decision  aligned  with  WHO  guidance to  capture  contemporary  evidence  for  POC  tests  and health  literacy  definitions.  As  the  final  search  was  conducted in May 2023, the study is limited in its consideration of more recent evidence or developments in POC testing, and the findings should be interpreted as reflecting  the  evidence  base  up  to  that  date.  Although  newer studies  may  now  exist,  findings  remain  highly  relevant and  provide  a  valuable  foundation  for  understanding the role of modern POC testing in health literacy. Additionally, 11 full-text reports were unable to be retrieved, largely  due  to  being  abstract-only  or  presented  in  nonwritten formats. While these reports may lack sufficient methodological  detail  for  full  appraisal,  their  exclusion may  still  introduce  retrieval  bias  and  contribute  to  the narrow evidence base identified.

Meta-analysis was not feasible due to variability between interventions, disease focus, measurement tools, and population characteristics. Outcomes were assessed using  diverse  instruments,  ranging  from  validated  tools (e.g.  PAM-13) to study-developed questionnaires, qualitative  interviews,  and  clinical  markers  of  adherence  or control.  This  mix  of  validated  and  self-reported  proxy measures introduces differences in measurement rigour, further  limiting  comparability  across  studies.  Despite this, shared self-reported proxy outcomes such as patient knowledge, confidence, engagement, and behaviour change  enabled  narrative  synthesis  via  the  framework of WHO health literacy actions. Designs were predominantly cross-sectional or short-term, with limited longitudinal  follow-up.  Furthermore,  equity  indicators  were inconsistently reported, limiting disaggregated analysis.  Although  age  and  sex  variables  were  consistently reported,  ethnicity  and  socioeconomic  indicators  were often absent, restricting the ability to generalise findings to  diverse  populations.  These  limitations  highlight  the need for more consistent measurements using validated health  literacy  instruments,  longer  follow-ups,  and  systematic equity reporting in future work.

While risk of bias assessment using the MMAT demonstrated generally strong methodological quality across the  included studies, several limitations were also identified.  Several  studies  did  not  adequately  account  for potential  confounders,  making  it  difficult  to  isolate  the contribution  of  POC  testing.  Two  studies  did  not  meet the  criterion  for  appropriate  interpretation  of  results based  on  the  data  provided  [27,  29],  however  this  was addressed  by  considering  only  the  raw  outcome  data in  the  synthesis.  One  study  did  not  meet  the  criterion for  low  non-response bias [30], potentially affecting the representativeness of its findings. Although these issues were  present  in  a  minority  of  studies,  they  may  reduce confidence in some reported associations and should be considered when interpreting the overall strength of the evidence base.

## Knowledge gaps and future directions

This  review  identified  a  small  number  of  recent  studies that  investigated  NCD-related  POC  testing  and  health literacy.  POC  measurements  included  diabetes/glucose, lipid  panels,  and  coagulation,  with  the  majority  conducted  in  populations  with  diabetes.  Findings  therefore are  not  generalisable  across  all  NCD  contexts.  Cohorts were geographically limited to eight countries, with only one of the thirteen included studies representing a LMIC (India). No studies were identified from Africa, which is a  major limitation given the disproportionate burden of NCDs in LMICs [2]. In LMICs, POC testing has particular potential to reduce inequities in the diagnosis, and subsequent  prevention,  treatment,  and  care  of  NCDs. In  settings  with  limited  resources,  household  funds  can be  quickly  depleted  by  NCD  care  and  treatment,  furthering  socioeconomic  inequities  [2].  The  introduction of  high-impact  interventions  tailored  for  implementation in LMICs is therefore imperative. Expanding future research to include diverse geographies (e.g. LMICs), as well  as  broader  POC  tests  (e.g.  chronic  respiratory  disease), will strengthen understanding and generalisability, particularly in priority populations.

Although some studies utilised validated Patient Activation  Measures  or  assessment  of  biomarker  changes, no identified studies applied specific health literacy measurement tools for outcome assessment. As a key determinant of health, future studies should adopt the use of global  health  literacy  instruments,  such  as  the  Health Literacy  Questionnaire  (HLQ)  [36],  to  generate  more targeted  data  and  improve  between-study  comparisons. Furthermore, longitudinal designs and clarity in reporting equity indicators would  benefit future research. Future work may also consider how system-level implementation and responsiveness in scaling up POC testing influence individual health literacy outcomes.

## Conclusions

The findings from this review suggest that POC testing for  NCD  measures,  specifically  in  diabetes  and  cardiovascular contexts, may support health literacy outcomes. POC  measures  were  limited  to  lipids  and  coagulation, and primarily blood glucose and HbA1c.  Reported interventions  were  associated  with  proxy  measures  of health  literacy,  including  an  enhanced  understanding of health, motivation to adopt positive lifestyle changes, greater medication adherence, increased confidence, and improved health markers. Despite this, current research in LMICs, where the burden of NCDs is particularly high, is limited. To enhance this understanding, further studies should prioritise the relationship between health literacy and POC testing for NCDs in underdeveloped regions.
