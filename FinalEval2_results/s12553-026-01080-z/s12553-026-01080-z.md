## A patient-centered approach to inclusive AI in healthcare: potential benefits, challenges, and recommendations

## Abstract

Purpose Artificial  Intelligence  (AI)  has  transformed  various  sectors,  including  healthcare,  where  it  holds  promises  for improving early detection, personalized care, and decision-making. However, despite its potential, AI systems often exacerbate health inequalities, particularly among underrepresented communities, due to biased algorithms, limited accessibility, and data exclusion. This current study aims to examine inclusive AI in healthcare grounded on a patient-centered approach. Methods We conducted a literature review following PRISMA 2020 guidelines, searching across online databases for publications from diverse geographic and cultural contexts. Articles were assessed for inclusion based on specific criteria, and data on healthcare, AI type, inclusivity frameworks, implementation methods, and SWOT analysis were extracted. The quality and risk of bias of included articles were assessed with the JBI Checklist for Expert Opinion; and the PROBAST tool. Results Findings from the study suggest that various AI types were discussed, including machine learning, chatbots, and convolutional neural networks. Several key frameworks for ensuring inclusive AI were reported in the included articles, such as ACCEPT-AI, Bias Mitigation, and human rights values frameworks. However, some articles did not mention specific inclusivity frameworks. Our findings suggest that AI in healthcare has the potential to improve health outcomes but must be implemented inclusively and transparently to avoid exacerbating inequalities.

Conclusions This review emphasizes the need for ethical, regulated, and collaborative approaches to mitigate bias, enhance accessibility, and ensure patient-centered solutions. Future efforts should focus on stakeholder-inclusive design and stan - dardized frameworks for equitable AI in healthcare.

Registration This literature review was not registered.

Similarly,  over  the  last  decade AI  is  now  being  deployed in the healthcare sector in various fields of medicine such as clinical decision support, drug discovery, medical imaging  analysis  prognostication  of  diseases  or  disease  outcomes, protein modeling, personalized medicine, etc [1, 3]. Advances in AI technologies hold great promise for improving early screening and detection methods, and improving or informing personalized healthcare outcomes [4]. These technologies  have  the  potential  to  analyze  large,  multidimensional datasets spanning user, clinical and other types of data in ways that enable informed decision-making, targeted interventions and precision care [5].

Irrespective  of  the  huge  potential  of  AI  in  healthcare its  use has paradoxically increased health inequalities and exclusion  especially  amongst  unrepresented  communities, race, gender, and ethnic minorities [6]. This is because AI algorithms and models that analyze large amounts of useror clinically-generated health data tend to exclude data from certain  user  populations  [6].  Moreover,  there  are  growing concerns that AI systems may further exacerbate inequalities in healthcare due to inconsistent accessibility, the lack of affordability, and biased AI algorithms and models [ 5]. This is in contrast to an inclusive society that offers equal opportunities  for  all  individuals  irrespective  of  their  age, race, gender, or social class [7, 8]. Inclusion refers to the degree to which individuals feel integrated or subjectively accepted  within  society.  In  the  context  of  this  study,  an inclusive healthcare system ensures equitable access for all [8]. Inclusive design focusses on developing services, environments, and products that accommodate individuals with diverse characteristics, needs, and abilities [9].

In the emerging era of digitalization and AI in healthcare, this means that digital health solutions, including AI-based health  technologies,  need  to  be  designed  with  a  patientcentered approach, so that users from different backgrounds and with diverse needs can interact with them seamlessly and benefit equally. Thus, it is essential to explore strategies for bridging the technological divide, particularly in underserved regions, to address disparities faced by patients experiencing social disadvantages and technological barriers [5, 10]. Equally important is the development of inclusive AI systems  (e.g. AI  systems  designed  to  mitigate  disparities across gender, race, and socioeconomic status) tailored to diverse patient groups, considering factors such as language discordance  between  healthcare  providers  and  patients  to mitigate  technological  exclusion  in  healthcare  [10].  This includes developing AI systems that promote better understanding  and  trust  between  patients  and  healthcare  providers [10]. The ethical guidance developed by the World Health  Organization  in  2021  emphasizes  the  importance of  inclusivity,  transparency,  and  accountability  in  AI  for healthcare [11].

An increasing number of published reviews on the use of  AI  in  healthcare  focus  primarily  on  the  technological advancements  and  algorithmic  performance  om  various fields, such as cardiology, dentistry, surgery, disease surveil - lance,  rehabilitation,  or  precision  medicine  among  others [12-17]. However, many of these AI systems, despite their technical sophistication, have been shown to perpetuate or even exacerbate health disparities due to the use of biased data or to design processes that overlook underrepresented groups. For example, several AI systems for dermatology have demonstrated significant racial bias [ 18, 19] There is also an ongoing research on how to mitigate biases in AI systems and address health disparities in healthcare through stakeholder involvement and human-centered AI principles [20]. Other studies emphasize the need for diverse datasets, fairness-conscious  algorithms,  and  regulatory  frameworks for inclusive AI. To the best of our knowledge, an overview on inclusive AI and strategies and frameworks to achieve 'inclusivity  in  AI'  in  healthcare  is  still  missing.  Where inclusivity in AI refers to the development and implementation of AI systems or models that are fair, accessible, and useful  to  all,  irrespective  of  gender,  background,  race,  or ability. In the context of this study inclusivity in AI aims to address or reduce algorithmic bias that may exist in diverse datasets. Thereby ensuring equitable access to AI systems or models, as well as the inclusion of diverse teams during the development of these AI systems or models.

As  suggested  by  the  World  Health  Organization  [11], who emphasizes the importance of inclusivity, transparency, and accountability in AI for healthcare [11]. Our literature review aims to provide an overview of scientific research on the application of inclusive AI technologies in healthcare, highlighting their reported strengths, weaknesses, opportunities and threats. Our study was guided by the following research questions:

- What are the facets of inclusive AI, and what are the recommended strategies, solutions, and regulatory frameworks  needed  to  realize  inclusive  AI  technologies  in healthcare?
- What are the application areas where inclusive AI technologies have been investigated?
- What are the potential benefits (strengths and benefits) of inclusive AI technologies within the healthcare domain?
- What  are  the  potential  challenges  (weaknesses  and threats) of inclusive AI technologies within the healthcare domain?

Overall, in summary this study contributes to the existing body of knowledge by examining the application areas of inclusive AI technologies in healthcare, assessing their benefits  and  challenges,  exploring  the  facets  of  inclusivity  in AI, and most importantly, summarizing the proposed strategies, solutions and regulatory frameworks needed to achieve truly inclusive AI in healthcare. An additional objective of this study is to shape the AI research agenda for improving health inclusion when deploying AI systems among patients from  diverse  and  disadvantaged  communities.  Evidence from this study highlights the intersection of inclusive AI with  systemic  bias  and  healthcare  disparities,  examining how AI-driven inequities affect low-income groups, people with disabilities, communities of color, minorities, and other disadvantaged populations.

## 2  Methods

In this study, a literature review was adopted as the chosen research approach by carrying out a systematic review with conceptual synthesis, following the PRISMA 2020 guidelines [21], as seen in Appendix 3. We searched six databases (PubMed, PsycINFO, CINAHL, Academic Search Premier, IEEE Xplore, and Scopus) for publications that included the keyword 'Inclusive AI' in their abstract. In this study we prioritize  the  literature  to  explicitly  addressing  'inclusive AI' in healthcare as this area has not been well addressed in prior studies.  Accordingly, we used only the keyword 'Inclusive AI' 'strategies', 'solutions', 'regulatory frameworks', 'applications', 'benefits', 'challenges', AND 'bias', 'fair - ness', 'equity', OR 'diversity' AND 'healthcare' to ensure the search captured publications where inclusivity was an explicit and central focus. The search was conducted on the 26th of September 2024. The search was carried out again on the 16th of April 2026 after extending the search keyword based on the research questions being examined in this study to improve the literature. The full search strategy can be found in Appendix 1. We uploaded all identified refer - ences to EndNote, removed duplicates, and then uploaded the remaining references to Rayyan.

## 2.1  Eligibility and selection process

In  order  to  assess  eligibility,  two  authors  (EG  and  KD) independently screened the titles and abstracts of all identified articles. In the second step, two authors (EG and AJB) independently assessed the eligibility of the selected articles from the first step by reviewing their full text. Any disagree - ments were discussed with a third author (KD) until consensus was reached.

We considered the following eligibility criteria:

## Inclusion criteria :

- Articles  addressing  inclusive AI  in  the  context  of healthcare.
- Written in any language and published in any year.

## Exclusion criteria :

- Publications for which only the abstract is available (full text not available).
- Study protocols, reviews, or protocols of systematic reviews.
- Editorials,  letters  to  editor,  erratums,  corrigenda, correction,  comments, retracted articles, responses (Re: ), proceedings, books, etc.

## 2.2  Data items and data extraction

All  selected  articles  were  included  in  qualitative  synthesis  and  were  divided  between  two  authors  (EG  and  KD), with each author extracting data from half of the articles. After which further discussion was carried out among the three  authors  during  data  extraction. The  following  information was extracted from each study: Healthcare context (e.g.,  condition,  medical  specialty);  Type  of  AI  solution (e.g., AI-based prediction algorithm, natural language processing, chatbot); Use of frameworks and/or guidelines to ensure inclusivity in AI; Specific methods for implementing inclusivity within the AI solution; and the discussed SWOT data  addressing  the  strengths,  weaknesses,  opportunities, and threats of inclusive AI solutions. The classification of discussed  topics  into  SWOT  categories  was  based  on  the characteristics highlighted in each article. When the categorization was explicitly stated by the authors, it was directly adopted. When not clearly reported, positive characteristics already in place and described positively were classified as Strengths, while positive characteristics expected to occur were  classified  as  Opportunities.  Negative  characteristics that have already occurred were classified as Weaknesses, and negative characteristics anticipated to occur were classified as Threats. These classifications were performed by the author responsible for extracting the data.

From the results, we derived facets of inclusive AI solutions  in  healthcare,  formulated  recommendations  addressing these facets to achieve inclusive AI in healthcare and formulated a research agenda.

## 2.3  Critical appraisal and risk of bias assessment

We conducted a critical appraisal and risk of bias assessment of all included articles. Viewpoints and expert opinion papers were assessed using the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist  for  Expert  Opinion,  and  articles  involving  the development  or  testing  of AI/ML  models  were  appraised using the PROBAST tool.

## 3.2  Critical appraisal of the included articles

All 17 expert opinion articles met the JBI Checklist criteria for expert opinion, with unanimous 'Yes' ratings across the  first  five  domains:  clear  identification  of  the  opinion source,  author  standing  in  the  field  of  expertise,  central focus on the interests  of  the  relevant  population  (marginalized, racial, and ethnic minorities in AI-health contexts), logically  defended  arguments  supporting  the  conclusions drawn, and comprehensive reference to the extant literature

<!-- image -->

(See Table 2). For the sixth domain, 10 articles reported no significant  incongruence  with  existing  sources,  while  the remaining  seven  explicitly  acknowledged  and  logically defended  noted  incongruences,  including  the  dominance of  high-income  country  perspectives  requiring  adaptation to low- and middle-income settings, the integration of nonWestern ethical frameworks such as Ubuntu, the limitations of  individual-responsibility  models  in  distributed  AI  systems, gaps in the literature, the generally optimistic framing of AI and AgeTech, and author's optimism despite current harms associated with AI literature (See Table 2).

Using PROBAST, the study of Ekellem et al. [28] was judged to have a high risk of bias across specially in the analysis domain due to overfitting, lack of proper validation, and small sample size. Additional concerns were identified in participant selection and outcome definition, limiting the generalizability and reliability of the findings (See Table 3).

## 3.3  Context: healthcare domains and types of AI solutions

Regarding  the  healthcare  context  of  AI  use,  six  articles referred to AI for healthcare in general [1, 6, 9, 22, 23, 31]; while  the  rest  focused  on  specific  topics,  such  as  AI  for adolescent and young adult health [25], AI in global child health with an emphasis on low- and middle-income countries [33], in newborn screening programs [32], for active aging [26], as a means of accessing healthcare services [8], in cancer care [5, 29], radiology [27], dermatology [28], in HIV  applications  [30],  for  mental  health  among  students [24], and for people with cognitive disabilities [34].

Eight articles discussed AI in general [6, 8, 22, 23, 25, 29-31], seven AI and machine learning [1, 5, 9, 26, 27, 32, 33], one focused on adaptive and personalized explanation systems  for AI  interactions  [34],  one  discussed  a  chatbot [24],  and  one  article  referred  to  convolutional  neural  networks (CNNs) in AI applications [28].

## 3.4  Frameworks to ensure inclusivity, methods and definitions of inclusive AI

Several frameworks and guidelines supporting inclusive AI were reported in the included articles. One article referred to the ACCEPT-AI, SPIRIT-AI, and CONSORT-AI frameworks  [33].  Another  article  discussed  a  Bias  Mitigation Framework and ethical AI design principles [8], while one highlighted  the  FAIR,  FACT,  and  RE-AIM  frameworks [31].  Maheshwari  et  al.  referred  to  a  framework  of  institutional epistemic trust [32], and Israni et al. mentioned a human rights values framework [25]. Tielman et al. outlined the  IEEE  7000  standard  for  ethical  system  design,  W3C Accessibility  Guidelines,  and  a  socio-cognitive  engineering method [34]. Doo and McGinty referred to the 'digital determinants of health' sociotechnical framework [27], while  Dekker  et  al.,  mentioned  the  Braun  and  Matthes framework  for  chatbot  classification  and  several  technol - ogy acceptance theories [24]. Ekellem and Köhler referred to  GUI  (Graphical  User  Interface)  principles  [28],  and Denecke  et  al.  discussed  Just-in-Time Adaptive  Intervention design [9]. One article highlighted the principles of distributive justice [5], and another one referred to Sustainable Development Goal 17 of the United Nations ('Partnership for the Goals') [22]. Rubeis et al. mentioned the Social Justice Framework for Bridging the Digital Divide and Health Equity Impact Assessment [26], while one article discussed the Ubuntu philosophy [6]. Four articles [1, 23, 29, 30] did not mention any specific framework or guideline for ensur - ing inclusive AI.

Many AI models inherit biases due to the underrepresentation of diverse populations in datasets and reliance on historically biased data. This leads to unfair and less inclusive outcomes. Aspects contributing to these weaknesses mentioned in the papers are lack of diverse data, over-reliance on datasets from high-income countries or bias in clinical care. Another weakness is the lack of standardized frameworks, ethical guidelines, and clear user consent processes. This  hinders AI  adoption  by  mistrust  and  concerns  about fairness. AI decision-making is often a 'black box,' making it  difficult  to  understand  and  correct  biases.  Lack  of  user input integration further limits inclusivity. AI systems can perpetuate  cultural  insensitivity,  breach  privacy,  and  lead to excessive dependence on technology for essential decision-making. Disparities in access to AI-driven tools such as accessibility barriers or limited adoption in low-resource areas create gaps in inclusivity, particularly for underserved populations.  The  most  frequently  mentioned  weaknesses were bias and data limitations, cited in 16 articles, emphasizing how underrepresentation in datasets and reliance on biased historical data limit inclusivity and fairness [1, 5, 6, 8, 9, 22, 23, 25-31, 33, 34].

We  also  identified  several  opportunities  for  inclusive AI.  Inclusive AI  can  play  a  transformative  role  in  reducing  healthcare  and  digital  inequities  by  improving  access for  marginalized  populations  and  addressing  social  determinants  of  health.  By  applying  inclusive  design  frameworks, considering regulatory frameworks and participatory approaches during the development, inclusive AI can foster trust and transparency of AI solutions. Co-design methodologies and international collaborations in aggregating data sets to be used in AI systems may improve AI fairness. Educating stakeholders on AI applications and limitations can improve adoption and reduce skepticism. AI can improve in accuracy and personalization by refining models, improving data  collection,  and  leveraging  privacy-preserving  learning  techniques.  Among  opportunities,  we  identified  two big  topics  as  the  most  commonly  discussed:  the  potential in advancing health equity and innovations in design [5, 9, 22, 24, 27, 28, 30, 33] and the need of inclusive frameworks and policy [1, 6, 8, 25, 27, 28, 31, 32], each discussed in 8 articles.

## 4  Discussion

## 4.1  Summary of findings

A total of 18 articles were included in this literature review, all published between 2020 and 2024. The critical appraisal indicated that the majority of articles included were expert opinion papers meeting key JBI quality criteria, suggesting a  generally  strong  conceptual  and  theoretical  foundation. However,  the  predominance  of  expert  opinion  highlights a limited availability of empirical evidence in the field of inclusive  AI  in  healthcare.  These  articles  focused  on  AI applications  in  health,  including  general  healthcare,  cancer care, mental health, and specific conditions like cogni - tive disabilities. These articles referred to various AI types, including  machine  learning,  chatbots,  and  convolutional neural networks. Multiple frameworks for ensuring inclusive AI were reported, such as ACCEPT-AI, Bias Mitigation, and human rights values frameworks. However, some articles did not mention specific inclusivity frameworks. The SWOT analysis highlights improved accessibility and inclusivity as a key strength, while the most frequently identified weakness is bias and data limitations. Among the opportunities, advancing health equity and fostering innovation in design,  frameworks,  and  policy  were  the  most  discussed topics. The most commonly cited threat was the potential for AI to exacerbate inequities, excluding some race, certain gender,  thereby  widening  health  disparities. We  identified several recurring themes related to the participatory health approach that AI technologies are supporting. These themes center on empowering patients, improving communication and relationships, promoting shared decision-making, and fostering equitable healthcare practices. These application areas support the need to design AI technologies inclusively in  order  not  to  exclude  individuals  from  benefiting  from healthcare services delivered by AI technologies [4].

## 4.2  Facets of inclusive AI in healthcare

Figure  3  depicts  the  facets  of  inclusive  AI  in  healthcare identified  from  the  literature  [ 35-38 ].  The  identified  fac - ets of inclusive AI in healthcare as they can be concluded from the SWOT analysis results, reflect its core principles and challenges (see Fig. 3). Inclusive AI is multi-faceted, addressing  accessibility,  equity,  usability,  diversity,  mitigating  inequalities,  and  skills  development.  These  facets underscore  the  importance  of  inclusive  design  of AI  systems in healthcare, as it is important to ensure that all people have access to healthcare services and nobody will be excluded from healthcare services because of non-inclusive design  of  the  technology  or  missing  skills  of  interacting with the AI technologies. Nyariro et al. [35] recognized the need to study to what extent equity, diversity and inclusion is  integrated  throughout  the  lifecycle  of AI  in  healthcare with results of the review still pending [35]. The relevance of usability of digital tools in healthcare has already been perceived  as  relevant  for  mHealth  solutions.  This  fact  is supported by the availability  of  multiple  tools  for  assessing usability in mHealth solutions (e.g. Mobile App Rating Scale  [36],  mHealth  App  Usability  Questionnaire)  [37]. Future work should study whether existing tools for usability  assessment  are  suited  or  require  adaptation  to  ensure usability in AI technologies. Reducing inequalities will only be possible if AI technologies are designed to avoid exacerbating health inequalities. While bias in AI technologies has been assessed and studied already [38 ], effective strategies to address and overcome biased outcomes of these systems are still lacking.

## 4.3  Facilitators and challenges of inclusive AI in healthcare

Key facilitators  (strengths  and  opportunities)  of  inclusive AI technologies in healthcare include technological innovation and advances such as increased access to smartphones, advances  in  natural  language  processing  [39],  machine learning  [40],  and  generative  AI,  all  of  which  improve accessibility and inclusivity. These technologies also contribute significantly to advancing benefits health equity by empowering patients, enabling personalized medicine, optimizing workflows, and creating opportunities for preventive and proactive care [41].

<!-- image -->

Potential challenges (weaknesses and threats) of inclusive AI technologies within the healthcare domain include the need for diverse data sets, and their potential to exacerbate inequities.  The  development  of  inclusive AI  technologies relies on access to diverse datasets that reflect the complex - ity of real-world populations [1]. Ensuring that these data sets are representative and inclusive is crucial for developing effective and unbiased AI systems. Another challenge is  cultural  competence  and  sensitivity.  Inclusive AI  technologies  must  be  designed  with  cultural  competence  and sensitivity  in  mind,  taking  into  account  the  diverse  needs and preferences of patients from different backgrounds [ 10]. This  includes  ensuring  that  AI-powered  decision  support systems are aware of cultural differences in health beliefs and  practices.  Further  research  is  needed  to  evaluate  the effectiveness and equity of inclusive AI technologies in dif - ferent application areas, ensuring that these technologies are having a positive impact on health outcomes and reducing disparities.

Findings from our review align with the 'digital determinants of health' framework [27] which emphasizes how technological  design,  accessibility,  and  governance  shape health  equity  outcomes.  The  threats  we  have  identified, such as bias in algorithms, underrepresentation of marginalized groups, and unequal infrastructure, reflect the proposed key digital determinants (e.g., inequitable access to digital tools, varied digital literacy, and systemic bias embedded in technology development) [27]. Likewise, the opportunities identified in our review, such as co-design methodologies, international collaborations, and the adoption of inclusive frameworks,  can  provide  specific  approaches  on  how  to address these determinants. Our review also places inclusive AI within the discussion of how digital factors affect health  and  therefore  emphasizing  the  need  to  integrate socio-technical  and  equitable  perspectives  in  the  design, implementation  and  governance  of AI-based  technologies in healthcare. Our findings also support related approaches, such as the Social Justice framework for bridging the digital divide as suggested by Rubeis et al. [26], and the Human Rights values framework used by Israni et al. [25], highlighting  the  need  for AI  systems  that  are  both  technically robust and equity-driven.

To  ensure  the  long-term  usability  and  adaptability  of inclusive  AI  systems,  continuous  refinement  and  regu - lar updates are essential. It is crucial to engage in discussions that explore sustainable strategies for maintaining and improving inclusivity over time.

The  critical  appraisal  of  the  included  articles  indicates that  the  evidence  base  is  primarily  composed  of  expert opinion  articles  that  met  established  JBI  quality  criteria, suggesting  a  generally  strong  conceptual  foundation  but limited  empirical  validation.  However,  the  predominance of  expert-driven  contributions  highlights  a  gap  in  systematically evaluated, data-driven evidence in the field of inclusive AI in healthcare. The analysis also reveals a wide range of frameworks and guidelines that support inclusive AI  in  healthcare,  each  addressing  different  aspects  of  AI implementation. These frameworks include ethical design principles,  bias  mitigation  strategies,  institutional  trust, accessibility  considerations,  social  equity  and  regulatory compliance.  The  presence  of  multiple  frameworks  suggests a strong interdisciplinary approach but also indicates a potential need for standardization or harmonization. Several  frameworks  specifically  focus  on  ensuring  ethical AI development  and  mitigating  biases.  The  Bias  Mitigation Framework  and  ethical  AI  design  principles  underscore the importance of addressing algorithmic bias and fairness. Similarly,  the  Social  Justice  Framework  for  Bridging  the Digital Divide, the Human Rights Values Framework, and principles  of  distributive  justice  emphasize  the  need  for equity, fairness, and the protection of marginalized communities.  Some frameworks align with internationally recognized standards, such as the IEEE 7000 standard for ethical system design, the W3C Accessibility Guidelines, and technology acceptance theories.

These frameworks provide structured methodologies for AI  development,  ensuring  that AI-driven  healthcare  solutions comply with global accessibility and ethical standards. Frameworks like ACCEPT-AI, SPIRIT-AI, and CONSORTAI  are  tailored  specifically  for AI  applications  in  clinical and healthcare settings, ensuring transparency and rigor in AI-driven  medical  interventions.  Some  frameworks  focus on  cultural  and  philosophical  perspectives,  such  as  the Ubuntu philosophy, which emphasizes the interconnectedness  of  individuals  and  their  communities.  The  inclusion of  Sustainable  Development  Goal  17  (Partnership  for  the Goals) highlights the importance of cross-sector collaboration and global cooperation in developing inclusive AI solutions. This underscores the need for partnerships between healthcare  institutions,  policymakers,  AI  developers,  and community stakeholders.

Although  we  identified  a  variety  of  ethical  and  inclu - sive  frameworks,  each  was  mentioned  only  once  across the reviewed articles, indicating limited adoption or awareness within the field. Frameworks like Ubuntu philosophy, used  by Amugongo  et  al.  [6],  which  emphasize  community  and  interconnectedness,  may  be  underutilized  due  to a  lack  of  familiarity,  limited  interdisciplinary  integration, or  challenges  in  applying  culturally  specific  philosophies within predominantly technology-driven research. Greater efforts are needed to promote and adapt diverse frameworks to  enhance  the  inclusivity  and  ethical  grounding  of AI  in healthcare.  In  summary  this  study  examines  the  facets  of inclusive AI, and identifies application areas, potential chal - lenges,  recommended strategies, solutions, and regulatory frameworks needed to realize inclusive AI technologies in fostering patient-centered healthcare.

## 4.5  Research agenda for improving health inclusion when deploying AI systems

AI-based  tools  are  being  adopted  by  health  practitioners. In contrast, evidence from the literature suggests that most patients are still hesitant due to several factors such as privacy, data security, risk of misinformation, trust, etc [30]. The  existence  of  these  factors  may  decrease  fairness  and inclusiveness in healthcare when AI is deployed by patients [31 ].  Therefore,  this  section  identifies  factors  that  impact inclusive AI in healthcare as described below. The information or decision made by AI algorithms or systems related to patients' care is at times difficult to define in simple terms. Furthermore,  information  accuracy  is  another  factor  [30], prompting the capabilities of AI systems to deliver up-todate, adaptable, and standardized patient educational information. Thus, AI systems such as LLMs should consistently provide evidence-based information [10].

Furthermore, the transparency, interpretability, explainability, and visibility of AI systems is a factor that can influ - ence  inclusion  as  this  assess  how  clear AI  communicates information [30]. Transparency means that the data sources, methodologies, and potential limitations of AI systems and algorithms should be specified as most AI systems are black boxes [46]. In addition, there is need for fair and unbiased AI systems [30, 31]. Fairness ensures that AI algorithms treat everyone equally [6]. In developing AI systems, sensitive information used by AI may lead to data privacy breaches [30]. Another  factor  is  bias  in  responses  produced  by AI algorithms  [10 ],  which  can  substantially  affect  healthcare decisions,  particularly  for  marginalized  and  underserved communities [29].

Moreover, the lack of participatory design approaches by developers of AI systems is also a factor that limits the actualization of inclusive AI in healthcare. Participatory design promotes health equity equality, and justice. This is because it  creates  an  avenue  to  engage  marginalized  populations during  the  co-design,  development,  and  evaluation  of AI systems [1]. Participatory design of AI systems comprises of diverse stakeholders such as philosophers, computer scientists,  health  personnels,  patients,  etc  [1].  Gender  biases are another determinant that has impacted AI systems such as Large Language Models (LLM). As reported in the literature most LLM show prejudice in gender when it comes to healthcare [46]. This has also result in the unjust decision making, allocation of resources, and limited opportunities for  women  resulting  to  prejudices  and  stereotypes  for  all [46].

The  accessibility  of AI  systems  such  as  ChatGPT  can indeed help in bridging health information divide, especially in low-income communities. Although existing AI systems are not developed to diverse patient groups which results in disparity in healthcare. Thus, there is need for AI systems to develop systems that can accurately interpret and convey health related information across languages, thus facilitating understanding and trust between patients and healthcare providers [10].

The critical appraisal indicates that the evidence is primarily composed of expert opinion articles that met established  JBI  quality  criteria,  suggesting  a  generally  strong conceptual  foundation  but  limited  empirical  validation. This,  together  with  the  presence  of  at  least  one  empirical study with a high risk of bias identified through PROBAST, underscores the need for stronger methodological rigor and more robust empirical evidence to validate proposed inclusive  AI  frameworks  and  recommendations  in  real-world healthcare  settings.  Future  research  should  evaluate  the effectiveness of the frameworks considered in real-world AI implementation. By identifying  gaps  and  overlaps  among existing  frameworks,  a  more  unified,  comprehensive,  and adaptable framework could be developed for inclusive AI in healthcare. Such standardized framework could address the weaknesses and threats identified in our analysis. It would streamline AI development, ensure fairness, improve trust, and  foster  global AI  adoption  in  healthcare.  It  could  also address  critical  gaps  in  bias  mitigation,  accessibility,  and regulatory  alignment,  making  AI-driven  healthcare  solutions more equitable and effective for all patients, especially marginalized  communities.  Future  research  should  also prioritize  recommendations  based  on  their  feasibility  and potential impact. At short term, regular actions such as conducting  bias  audits,  implementing  transparency  measures, and enhancing data privacy measures could help to identify and mitigate unfairness in AI algorithms. At medium and long term, it would be desirable to adopt more user-centered approaches involving diverse stakeholders in order to create equitable AI systems.

## 4.6  Limitations of this study

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the results of this review. First, our selection of sources was mostly linked to publications that explored or contributed to 'inclusive AI'. Thus, there is a potential of omitting other relevant  studies  linked  to  patient-centered  healthcare  due to the terminologies used in the search strategy. Moreover, we did not include variations of these terms, additional keywords, or expand the search to the entire manuscript. As a result,  relevant  articles  that  used  alternative  terminology or did not explicitly include these keywords in the abstract may  have  been  overlooked. Additionally,  our  search  was limited to English-only databases and grey literature such as non-governmental reports on AI in low- and middle-income countries, which introduces a potential selection bias. However, the inclusion of articles published between 2020 and 2024 ensures that the review reflects the latest trends and developments in the field of inclusive AI in healthcare.  Addi - tionally, although our search was comprehensive and used several databases, it was limited to publications indexed in established databases, which may have excluded relevant or recent works published in other platforms such as preprints or institutional repositories.

Although two authors were involved in the data extraction  process,  the  data  extraction  was  not  performed  in duplicate, which may introduce potential bias or error. This increases  the  risk  of  missing  subtle  nuances  or  differing interpretations in coding the strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats (SWOT), potentially affecting the accu - racy of the findings.

Additionally, most included articles were expert opinion papers,  which,  despite  meeting  established  quality  criteria, may limit the strength of the evidence due to the lack of  empirical  validation.  Furthermore,  one  empirical  study assessed using PROBAST was found to have a high risk of bias, particularly in the analysis domain, which may affect the reliability and generalizability of its findings.

Moreover, the complexity of the topic and the overlap of certain concepts may have led to the omission of some relevant details or misclassifications. Further research should consider duplicate extraction to improve reliability. Moreover, issues related to the impact of AI in health care related to race and gender were not addressed in detail as this has been investigated in prior studies. While our review focused primarily on clinical AI applications, many of the identified strengths and challenges, such as bias, inclusivity, and transparency, can also be relevant to non-clinical AI domains like public health surveillance. Future research should explore how these findings translate to broader AI use cases further also considering the impact of AI use in healthcare related to race and gender.

## 5  Conclusions

In conclusion, the use of AI in healthcare offers significant opportunities  to  improve  health  and  well-being,  but  its implementation  must  be  approached  with  careful  consideration of inclusivity, transparency and equitable use. This study highlights the importance of inclusive AI in addressing  health  inequalities,  and  highlights  both  the  strengths and challenges associated with its use. Through an analysis of different AI applications and inclusivity frameworks, the  findings  emphasize  the  need  for  robust  strategies  to mitigate  bias,  improve  accessibility,  and  promote  patientcentered  healthcare  solutions.  The  SWOT  analysis  shows that while AI can advance health equity and innovation, it also has the potential to exacerbate existing inequalities if not properly regulated. To maximize the benefits of AI in healthcare, a participatory and inclusive approach is essential to ensure that all stakeholders - including patients, clinicians and policymakers - are involved in shaping AI-driven solutions. Going forward, the development and implementation of inclusive AI technologies must be guided by ethical principles, regulatory frameworks and interdisciplinary collaboration to create a healthcare system that is equitable, transparent and beneficial for all.

Future  efforts  will  focus  on  exploring  how  inclusive, patient-centered  AI  systems  can  be  designed  in  partnership with all key stakeholders in the healthcare sector and on  developing  a  standardized  framework  for  developing inclusive AI solutions in healthcare. Additionally, we will explore the impact of AI as related to health equity in society. Also, the identified 'facets of inclusive AI' (as seen in Fig. 3) will be expanded into a conceptual model, and then the  model  will  be  evaluated  using  qualitative  and  quantitative data collected using survey questionnaire and focus group interview.
