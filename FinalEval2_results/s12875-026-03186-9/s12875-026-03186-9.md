## Systematic review of direct access physiotherapy for musculoskeletal conditions in primary care: consequences for general practitioner workload, resource use, and organisation of care

Matthieu Fischer 1* , Emilie Bui 1 , Laure Besombes 2 and Mathilde François 1,2,3

## Abstract

Background Musculoskeletal disorders (MSDs) are frequent and disabling conditions in primary care and contribute substantially to general practitioners' (GPs) workload. Direct access (DA) to physiotherapy, allowing patients to consult physiotherapists without prior medical referral, has been proposed to streamline care, reduce delays, and optimise resource use. This systematic review synthesized the effects of DA on GPs' practice and the organisation of primary care for adults with MSDs.

Methods Following PRISMA 2020, we systematically searched 12 databases/platforms (MEDLINE/PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, CINAHL, PEDro, Web of Science, Scopus, Cairn.info, Érudit, ClinicalTrials.gov) and discovery platforms (ScienceDirect, Google Scholar), plus grey literature, from 15 July 2024 to 4 November 2025 (initial search 15 July-15 October 2024; updated search 1 January 2024-4 November 2025). Eligible studies assessed the consequences of DA to physiotherapy for MSDs in primary care versus GP-referred pathways (referral-based usual care). Two reviewers independently screened, extracted data, as well as appraised quality using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (observational studies), ROB 2.0 (RCTs), and graded certainty using GRADE at the study level. Outcomes included GP workload, imaging and medication prescriptions, clinical results, costs, and safety.

Results We included 21 studies (7 RCTs, 14 observational) totaling 90 401 patients (15 017 DA; 75 384 GP-referred). Across 15 studies, DA was associated with 10-30% fewer GP consultations. 14 studies reported reduced imaging and medication use under DA; effects on prescriptions were context-dependent. 14 studies showed non-inferior clinical outcomes, with small early advantages in some RCTs. 13 studies assessed costs, indicating neutral to reduced perepisode costs; two RCT-linked economic analyses were comparable or favorable to DA. No serious adverse events attributable to DA were reported (5 studies). GRADE certainty was moderate in 2, low in 12, and very low in 5 studies,

## Introduction

Musculoskeletal  disorders  (MSDs)  are  common  and disabling,  leading  to  chronic  pain,  functional  limitations,  and  loss  of  autonomy.  The  World Health Organization (WHO) identifies MSDs as a leading global cause of years lived with disability, affecting about 1.71 billion people (≈22%) [1, 2]. In France, MSDs are a major cause of occupational disease and consultation, with substantial economic impact [3, 4]. Management combines pharmacologic and non-pharmacologic treatments, patient education, and continuity of care [5, 6].

General  practitioners  (GPs)  are  often  the  first  contact point, who coordinate care pathways, and prescribe investigations/treatments [7-9]. Physiotherapists deliver evidence-based interventions -e.g., therapeutic exerciseto  restore  function  [5,  6].  Growing  GP  workload  and evolving physiotherapist  competencies  have  renewed interest  in  direct  access  (DA)  to  physiotherapy  without prior  medical  prescription.  Widely  adopted  abroad,  DA aims to shorten delays, reduce GP burden, and leverage physiotherapists' role;  several  studies  report  benefits  on pain,  function,  and  patient  satisfaction  [10-14].  Care under  DA  appears  comparable  to  physician-referred pathways on clinical outcomes and satisfaction [15-18]. However, effects on GPs and service organization remain under-studied.

Definitions  used  in  this  review:  Direct  Access  (DA) refers  to  patient  self-referral  to  a  physiotherapist  without prior medical prescription [19]; First Contact Physiotherapy (FCP) denotes a physiotherapist embedded as the  initial  MSK  contact  within  primary  care  pathways. The term "triage services" (structured telephone or digital assessment prior to in-person care) is provided here for conceptual orientation; studies involving physiotherapistled triage were captured through broader DA/FCP search terms (see Methods).

In  France,  DA  remains  limited  despite  recent  pilots/ experiments  [20,  21].  A  historically  physician-centered system  and  legal/cultural/organizational  barriers  constrain  diffusion  [21-23].  In  the  context  of  strained  primary care and the need for safe, accessible organizational innovation, DA warrants systematic evaluation.

## Objective

To assess the impact of DA to physiotherapy on general practice for adult MSDs. The primary outcome was GP workload (number/frequency of consultations). Secondary outcomes were prescriptions (medications, imaging), clinical outcomes (pain, function, quality of life), direct/ indirect  costs,  interprofessional  collaboration,  GP  satisfaction/perceptions, and adverse events.

## Methods

## Study design and reporting framework

This systematic review followed Preferred Reporting  Items  for  Systematic  Reviews  and  Meta-Analyses (PRISMA)  2020  [24].  In  accordance  with  PRISMA,  we prepared  outcome-specific  Summary  of  Findings  tables (SoF) that collate study-level results per outcome and are provided  as  Additional  Tables  S1-S7.  The  protocol  was prospectively registered on the Open Science Framework (OSF; ID 897ub; 15 August 2024, available at  h t t p s : / / o s f . i o/ 8 9 7 u b / ) [25]. The completed PRISMA 2020 checklist is available in Additional file 1.

## Search strategy

The  initial  search  was  conducted  from  15  July  2024  to 15  October  2024.  To  ensure  currency  of  evidence,  an updated search was performed from 1 January 2024 to 4 November 2025, capturing any late-indexed records from the  original  search  period  and  newly  published  studies.  Searches  covered  databases  (MEDLINE/PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, CINAHL, PEDro, Web of Science, Scopus,  Cairn.info, Érudit, ClinicalTrials. gov)  and  discovery  platforms  (ScienceDirect,  Google Scholar).  Grey  literature  was  identified  through  structured searches of Google  Scholar  (using predefined search equations consistent with the main database strategy), clinical trial registries (ClinicalTrials.gov and WHO ICTRP), and French-language platforms (Cairn.info, Érudit), which index professional and institutional literature not  systematically  covered  by  international  biomedical databases (see Additional file 2).

## Eligibility criteria (PICO framework)

Population: adults  (≥ 18  years)  with  non-specific  MSDs (i.e.,  musculoskeletal  conditions  without  identified  specific  pathology  such  as  fracture,  inflammatory  arthritis, or  malignancy)  managed  in  primary  care; Intervention: DA to physiotherapy. Comparator: GP-referred pathway (referral-based  usual  care); Primary outcome: GP  workload (number/frequency of GP consultations); Secondary outcomes: medication/imaging prescriptions, patientreported outcomes (pain, function, QoL), direct/indirect costs,  safety; Designs  included: RCTs  and  observational comparative  studies  (cohort,  case-control,  quasi-experimental).  This  combination  was  chosen  to  capture  both efficacy data from experimental designs (RCTs) and realworld organizational impacts (observational studies) often  unavailable  in  experimental  settings.  Systematic reviews  were  excluded  from  the  synthesis  and  used  for context only. Qualitative-only reports, case series, protocols,  and  non-peer-reviewed documents were excluded. A consolidated PICO framework mapping populations,  interventions/comparators,  and  outcomes  across included studies is presented in Additional file 3.

## Study selection

Two  reviewers  (MaF  and  EB)  independently  screened titles/abstracts and then full texts using Rayyan for webbased  screening  and  duplicate  detection  [26];  disagreements  were  resolved  by  consensus.  For  the  updated search (1 January 2024-4 November 2025), a pragmatic single-reviewer  approach  (MaF)  was  adopted  at  title/ abstract  level  given  the  short  timeframe.  To  assess  reliability,  a  random  20%  sample  ( n =  13/65)  was  independently  screened  by  a  second  reviewer  (EB),  yielding excellent  inter-rater  agreement  (Cohen's  κ = 0.91).  All potentially eligible records were subsequently assessed at full text by both reviewers independently.

## Data extraction

Data were extracted using a standardised form adapted from  the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions (version 6.3) [27], with pre-specified units/ scales  (e.g.,  VAS  0-10  or  0-100,  ODI  0-100,  MSK-HQ 0-56,  EQ-5D  index)  recorded  as  reported.  Extracted items  included  authors,  year,  country,  design,  sample, intervention/comparator, outcomes, measurement tools, sources of funding, declared conflicts of interest, and key quantitative results. Participants were counted once per unique  study  or  cohort  to  avoid  double  counting  when the same cohort was reported across linked publications (e.g.,  primary  study and linked economic evaluation) or contributed data to multiple outcomes. For transparency, all  study-level  quantitative  results  used  to  populate  the SoF are reproduced verbatim in Additional Tables S1-S7, with units and time windows preserved. Two reviewers

(MaF and EB) independently extracted data and reconciled discrepancies by consensus.

## Risk of bias and methodological quality

Risk  of  bias  and  methodological  quality  were  assessed with  Risk  of  Bias  2.0  (ROB  2.0)  for  RCTs  [28]  and  the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) for observational studies [29]. Ratings were harmonized (low/moderate/high). Risk of  bias  assessments  were  performed  independently  by two reviewers (MaF and EB) with consensus resolution. Detailed study-level quality appraisals are summarized in Additional file 4. A cross-study bias matrix (randomization, selection, performance, detection, attrition, reporting,  publication,  language,  setting,  and  confounding)  is provided in Additional file 5.

## Certainty of evidence

Outcome-level  certainty  was  graded  with  Grading  of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE): risk of bias, inconsistency, imprecision, publication  bias,  indirectness  [30];  Given  the  narrative synthesis without pooled estimates and the heterogeneity  of  outcomes  precluding  outcome-level  aggregation, GRADE judgements  were  applied  at  the  study  level  as inputs to the overall synthesis, consistent with guidance for reviews without meta-analysis. Economic evaluations published separately inherited the parent RCT's certainty rating.  Dual  independent  grading  (MaF  and  EB)  with consensus.

## Synthesis methods

Given  clinical  and  methodological  heterogeneity  across designs,  settings,  and  outcomes,  we  conducted  a  narrative  synthesis.  Where  available,  we  report  effect  measures  with  95%  CIs  and  p-values.  We  did  not  perform formal  small-study  or  publication-bias  tests  because  no meta-analysis  was  undertaken;  instead,  we  qualitatively appraised potential reporting/publication biases and reflected  these  concerns  within  GRADE.  Results  from secondary  research  (systematic  reviews)  were  used  for context  only  and  were  not  recombined  with  primary studies to avoid double counting.

## Ethical considerations

Since  only  published/public  data  were  used,  no  ethics approval was required. Protocol/eligibility was preregistered on OSF [25].

## Results

## Study selection

The  combined  searches  identified  7,264  records  (7,239 from databases and 25 from trial registries). After removal of duplicates, 5,003 unique records were screened.  The  initial  search  (15  July-15  October  2024)

contributed 6,811 records; the updated search (1 January 2024-4 November 2025) yielded 453 additional records but  no  further  eligible  primary  studies,  likely  reflecting evidence  base  stabilization  and  publication  lag.  After screening  and  eligibility  assessment,  21  studies  were included. The PRISMA 2020 flow diagram of study selection is presented in Fig. 1. The final sample comprised 7 randomised controlled trials (RCTs) and 14 observational studies.  Two economic evaluations published separately were linked to their respective parent RCTs for all quality and certainty assessments to avoid double counting.

## Study characteristics

A  total  of  90,401  adults  with  musculoskeletal  disorders (MSDs)  were  analyzed  (15,017  in  direct  access  [DA] models; 75,384 in GP-referred pathways). This figure represents unique participants per cohort; the higher total of 91,599  sometimes  derivable  from  supplementary  tables results  from  summing  sample  sizes  across  linked  publications  and  outcome-specific  reports  from  the  same underlying  cohorts  (see  Methods).  Most  studies  originated  from  the  UK,  Sweden,  and  North  America,  with one  French  pilot.  MSDs  mainly  included  low  back  and neck pain, osteoarthritis, and tendinopathies. DA models ranged  from  fully  autonomous  first-contact  physiotherapy to structured triage-based services.

Funding  for  12  of  the  17  included  reports  was  provided  by  public  or  non-profit  institutional  grants  (e.g., NIHR, Versus Arthritis, regional health services, or university grants). One study was funded by a combination of industry and professional association grants, and four reports  did  not  state  a  funding  source.  Regarding  competing  interests,  12  reports  explicitly  declared  no  conflicts  of  interest.  Two  reports  declared  minor  editorial conflicts  unlikely  to  influence  the  outcomes,  and  three reports did not include a competing interest statement.

## Primary outcome-GP workload

Fifteen studies assessed  GP  workload.  Most  studies reported a 10-30% reduction in GP consultations under DA  vs  GP-referral  [31-35];  where  available,  we  report 95% CIs/p-values and specify country/setting (e.g., NHS Scotland,  UK  primary  care).  UK  RCTs  confirmed  no increase  in  GP  visits  [36].  A  large  Scottish  programme estimated  1,739  GP-hours  saved  per  year  [32].  Specific quantitative examples:  lower  imaging  rates  (7.3% vs 13.6% in  an  RCT; p &lt;  0.001)  and  fewer  GP  visits  (0.3  vs 1.5  per  patient  in  an  RCT-linked  economic  evaluation) are consistent with these findings [34, 37]. Table 1 summarises the study-level findings for the primary outcome (GP  workload)  comparing  direct  access  (DA)  with  GPreferred care; most studies report a reduction in GP consultations under DA, typically around 10-30%.

## Secondary outcomes

## Prescriptions and imaging

Prescriptions Across 14 studies, DA was generally associated with lower medication use; several cohorts reported 50-85% fewer analgesic prescriptions, including non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and opioids [31, 39, 46, 47]. All medication-related outcomes are compiled in A dditional  file  7  in  Table  S2  (SoF-Medication  use: analgesics/NSAIDs/opioids). Corresponding GRADE ratings and study-level quality (ROB 2.0/NOS) are reported in Additional file 6 and Additional file 4. Some UK cluster contexts showed no reduction or slight increases where triage support was weak [36, 37].

Imaging Comparative  studies  reported  30-76%  lower imaging  when  DA  was  structured  [32-35].  Pragmatic RCTs  confirmed  lower  X-ray rates (7.3% vs 13.6%, p &lt;  0.001)  [38].  All  imaging  outcomes  in  Additional  file 7  in  Table  S3  (SoF-Imaging:  X-ray/advanced  imaging). Corresponding  GRADE  ratings  and  study-level  quality (ROB 2.0/NOS) are reported in Additional file 6 and Additional file 4.

## Clinical outcomes

Across 14 studies, DA showed clinical outcomes similar to  GP-referred  care  for  pain,  function  (Musculoskeletal Health  Questionnaire  [MSK-HQ],  Oswestry  Disability Index [ODI]), and quality of life (EuroQol 5-Dimension [EQ-5D],  12-Item  Short  Form  Health  Survey  [SF-12]) [36,  40,  41,  44].  Some  RCTs  suggested  modest  early benefits (e.g.,  -2.2  visual  analogue  scale  [VAS]  points  at 6  weeks)  [41],  with  no  sustained  differences  mid-term [40, 41, 48]; where available, mean differences with 95% CIs are reported. Study-level clinical results (scales, timepoints, mean differences with 95% CI/p when available) are presented in Additional file 7 in Table S4 (SoF-Clinical outcomes).

## Costs

Thirteen studies assessed economic outcomes. Economic analyses  from  observational  studies  indicated  neutral to  moderate cost  savings  under  direct  access,  with  perpatient  savings  of  ≈€200-€1,828  [31,  46,  47],  with  perspective (payer/NHS/societal), time horizon (per episode vs  follow-up),  and  price  year  reported  where  available. Two pragmatic RCTs found neutral to modestly reduced total  costs,  with  resource-use  components  detailed  by perspective and price year where available. Fewer GP visits (0.3 vs 1.5, p &lt;  0.001) and fewer imaging requests (9% vs  39%)  [36,  41]  were  highlighted.  One  trial  reported  a non-significant total-cost difference (-€364, p =  0.17) [41]. Itemized cost components and total-cost comparisons by perspective and price year are reported Additional file 7 in Table S5 (SoF -Costs: payer/NHS/societal).

## Safety

Across 90,401 patients, no serious adverse events attributable to DA were reported in RCTs [36, 38, 40, 41, 48]; five  studies  explicitly  assessed  safety.  An  observational study documented appropriate red-flag detection within the  service  evaluated  [42].  Safety  endpoints  (adverse effects,  red-flag  detection/referral)  are  summarized  in Additional file 7 in Table S6 (SoF-Safety).

## Collaboration and GP perceptions

No  studies  evaluated  GP  satisfaction,  perceived  quality of physiotherapy care, or interprofessional collaboration; This gap is documented in Additional file 7 in Table S7 (SoF-Collaboration  and  GP  perceptions),  which records the absence of comparative data.

## Risk of bias

Among RCTs, most domains were low to moderate risk on ROB 2.0; lack of blinding was inherent, with moderate attrition in two trials [36, 38, 40, 41, 48]. Observational studies showed moderate-high risk (selection, confounding, self-report) [35, 41, 42, 44, 46, 47]. A heatmap summarizing the risk-of-bias domains across included studies is presented in Fig. 2.

## Methodological quality

Observational NOS scores ranged 4-6/9 (low-moderate) [32-35, 39, 42, 44, 46, 47]. RCTs were generally of moderate methodological quality but had small sample sizes and few participating sites, with limited allocation concealment [36, 38, 40, 41, 48]. The distribution of methodological quality levels by appraisal tool (ROB 2.0, NOS) is displayed in Fig. 3.

## Certainty of evidence

Using GRADE (study level), 2 studies reached moderate certainty, 12 were low, and 5 very low, often downgraded for risk of bias, imprecision, and inconsistency. The certainty-of-evidence levels according to GRADE and study design are shown in Fig. 4.

## Overall synthesis

Evidence suggests DA reduces GP workload and resource use  (medications,  imaging)  while  showing  clinical  outcomes  similar  to  GP-referred  pathways  and  no  consistent signal of increased harms. Economic analyses from observational studies indicated neutral to moderate cost savings, though reliance on non-randomised data limits certainty.

Our  findings  align  with  international  trends  advocating  early  physiotherapy  access  for  MSDs  to  relieve  primary  care  [12,  19,  49-51]  and  with  UK  First  Contact Physiotherapy (FCP) models [36, 37, 44]. Limited long-term follow-up precludes firm conclusions on recurrence or delayed medical consumption [36, 37, 40].

## Implications for policy across healthcare systems

The findings of this review have differential implications depending  on  healthcare  system  architecture.  In  Beveridgean systems (e.g., UK NHS), direct access has been implemented  through  First  Contact  Practitioner  (FCP) models  embedded  within  general  practice,  with  physiotherapists  serving  as  first  point  of  contact  for  MSK complaints. The evidence synthesized here supports the continued expansion of such models, with demonstrated reductions  in  GP  workload  and  comparable  clinical outcomes.

In Bismarckian systems such as France and Germany, where physiotherapy traditionally  requires  medical  prescription and reimbursement is tied to physician referral, implementation faces additional regulatory and cultural barriers.  In  France,  despite  recent  regulatory  developments  -including  the  2023  Rist  law  introducing  limited direct access [20], the 2024 implementing decree expanding its scope within organised primary care settings [21], and the ministerial  order  of  25  June  2025  further  clarifying  eligible  conditions  and  professional  requirements [52]-,  DA  remains  limited  by:  (i)  legal  requirements  for medical  prescription;  (ii)  fee-for-service  reimbursement structures  not  adapted  to  first-contact  physiotherapy; (iii)  historical  professional  boundaries;  and  (iv)  variable physiotherapist training in differential diagnosis and redflag screening. Our findings suggest that, if these barriers were addressed, direct access could contribute to alleviating  the  well-documented pressures on French general practice, particularly in underserved areas.

In  liberal  healthcare  systems  (e.g.,  USA),  where  DA legislation  varies  by  state,  the  evidence  supports  unrestricted access models, with studies consistently showing lower costs and comparable outcomes. However, implementation considerations differ regarding insurance coverage and out-of-pocket costs for patients.

## Interpreting certainty of evidence for policy decisions

The predominance of low and very low GRADE certainty ratings across outcomes reflects the observational nature of most included studies, a limitation inherent to health services  research  where  randomization  is  often  unfeasible or ethically problematic. This "low certainty" rating should not be interpreted as "poor quality evidence" but rather as appropriate epistemic humility given the study designs available.

Importantly,  low  certainty  does  not  imply  that  policy action  should  be  deferred.  GRADE  guidance  explicitly  acknowledges  that  strong  recommendations  can be  made  despite  low  certainty  evidence  when:  (i)  the direction  of  effect  is  consistent  across  studies;  (ii)  the magnitude  of  potential  benefits  substantially  outweighs potential  harms;  and  (iii)  the  consequences  of  inaction The two studies achieving moderate  certainty for selected outcomes (GP workload proxies, costs) provide an anchor of higher-confidence evidence, while the convergent  findings  from  multiple  lower-certainty  studies across  diverse  healthcare  systems  provide  corroborating  support.  For  decision-makers,  this  triangulation  of consistent  findings  offers  a  robust  directional  signal  for policy,  even  if  precise  effect  magnitudes  remain  uncertain. Future pragmatic trials and well-designed prospective cohorts could strengthen certainty, but should not be prerequisites for cautious implementation with appropriate monitoring.

## Strengths and limitations

Strengths include PRISMA-guided methods, multi-database search with prospective update to November 2025, double independent screening/appraisal, and use of validated tools (ROB 2.0, NOS, &amp; GRADE). The breadth of health system contexts and organisational models covered also strengthens the external validity of the findings, particularly  for  health  services  research  and  policy-oriented  interpretation. Limitations include  heterogeneity of  DA/FCP/triage models and outcomes, predominance of  observational designs, small RCT samples, and short follow-up, which prevented a robust meta-analysis; potential publication/reporting biases are considered within the GRADE judgements.

Type of study

Priorities  include  pragmatic  RCTs  and  prospective cohorts  in  regulated  systems,  with  adequate  samples, long-term outcomes, and full economic evaluations [36, 37, 40, 41]; and studies on GP satisfaction, collaboration, and patient acceptability [15, 16]. Future research should also adopt mixed-methods designs to better capture contextual factors influencing  implementation,  including organisational readiness and interprofessional dynamics. Such approaches are essential to inform policy decisions in systems where DA is introduced incrementally rather than through wholesale reform.

## Implications for practice, organisation, and future research

DA appears safe and may alleviate GP consultation workload,  supporting  more  rational  imaging  and  prescribing [32-36,  38].  For  France,  scaling  would  require  structured  triage/protocols  [20,  22,  23],  interoperable  tools, explicit  collaboration  frameworks  [15,  16],  and  training in  differential  diagnosis  for  physiotherapists [49]; evaluation  within  community  health  centres  could  monitor real-world  impact  [21,  53,  54].  Beyond  clinical  safety, implementation should be viewed as a reconfiguration of care  pathways,  with  implications  for  professional  roles, accountability,  and  continuity  of  care  in  primary  care teams. Embedding DA within coordinated organisational models may therefore be as critical as regulatory change to ensure sustainable adoption.

## Conclusions

In  adults  with  MSDs,  direct  access  to  physiotherapy appears to reduce GP workload and resource use (medications,  imaging)  with  clinical  outcomes  similar  to  GPreferred pathways and no consistent signal of increased harms. Given the predominance of observational designs and  small  pragmatic  trials  in  the  included  evidence, future  research  should  prioritize  adequately  powered RCTs and long-term economic evaluations in regulated healthcare systems, alongside studies of GP acceptability and interprofessional collaboration.
