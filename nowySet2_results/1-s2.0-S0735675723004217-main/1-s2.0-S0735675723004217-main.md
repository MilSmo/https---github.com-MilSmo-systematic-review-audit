## Guideline-based and restricted fluid resuscitation strategy in sepsis patients with heart failure: A systematic review and meta-analysis

Ali Vaeli Zadeh, M.D. a,b,1, ⁎ , Alan Wong, M.D. b,1 , Andrew Carl Crawford, M.D. b , Elias Collado, M.D. a,b,2 , Joshua M. Larned, M.D. a,b,2

## Abstract

Objectives: To examine whether a fluid resuscitation strategy based on guidelines (at least 30 mL/kg IV crystalloids) vs. a restrictive approach with &lt;30 mL/kg within three hours affects in-hospital mortality in patients with sepsis and a history of heart failure (HF).

Data sources: On03/07/2023, we searched Embase, PubMed, and Scopus for peer-reviewed papers and abstracts using the PRISMA guidelines.

Study selection: The language was limited to English. Studies published since 2016 included if they had sepsis patients with a history of HF, or a subgroup of patients with HF, and in-hospital mortality data on these patients that did or did not meet the 30 mL/kg by 3 h (30 × 3) goal. Duplicate studies, studies that focused on a broader period than 3 h from the diagnosis of sepsis or without mortality breakdown for HF patients or with unrelated title/abstract, or without an IRB approval were excluded.

Data extraction: In-hospital mortality data was taken from the final studies for HF patients with sepsis who did or did not meet the 30 × 3 goal.

Data synthesis: The meta-analysis was performed using the Review Manager 5.4 program with ORs as the effect measure. The ProMeta program version 3.0 was used to evaluate the publication bias. Egger's linear regression and Berg and Mazumdar's rank correlation was used to evaluate the publication bias. The result was visually represented by a funnel plot. To estimate the proportion of variance attributable to heterogeneity, the I 2 statistic was calculated.

Results: The search yielded 26,069 records, which were narrowed down to 4 studies. Compared to those who met the 30 × 3 goal, the &lt;30 × 3 group had a significantly higher risk of in-hospital mortality (OR = 1.81, 95% CI = 1.13 - 2.89, P =0.01).

Conclusions: Restrictive fluid resuscitation increased the risk of in-hospital mortality in HF patients with sepsis. More rigorous research is required to determine the optimal fluid resuscitation strategy for this population.

The Third International Consensus Definitions for Sepsis and Septic Shock defines sepsis as life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection, with organ dysfunction being represented by a change in Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score by 2 or more from baseline [2].

The most recent 2018 Surviving Sepsis Campaign Bundle (SSCB) update considered intravenous (IV) fluid resuscitation as the cornerstone of sepsis treatment. This expert consensus statement recommended administration of at least 30 mL/kg of IV crystalloids within the first 3 h of recognition of sepsis when hypotension and/ or elevated lactate is present without exceptions for patients with preexisting comorbidities that may be affected by volume overload, including heart failure (HF) [3]. However, when it comes to volume resuscitation, sepsis and concomitant HF provide a particularly difficult clinical scenario. Several publications reported a link between positive fluid balance in septic patients and mortality [4-6]. On the other hand, adherence to a minimum 30 mL/kg bolus was not linked to increased mortality in a multicenter study comparing treatment before and after sepsis bundle implementation [4]. One multicenter observational study found that sepsis bundle implementation was associated with improved volume resuscitation and increased survival in patients with sepsis and concomitant HF [5] .

Recently the CLOVERS trial concluded that with sepsis-induced hypotension, the restrictive fluid strategy did not increase (or decrease) mortality or safety concerns before discharge on day 90 compared to the liberal fluid strategy [6]. These and other findings explain why some physicians are hesitant about ordering fluids for septic patients at higher risk of volume overload. The ideal resuscitation strategy is unknown, and whether early guideline-based volume resuscitation is appropriate for individuals with HF is controversial. One study showed that crystalloid was initiated significantly later in patients with HF or renal failure [7]. Other studies reported that patients having a prior medical history of HF were more than twice as likely to receive less fluid [8].

Although fluid resuscitation trials on patients with sepsis and a history of HF stratified by severity are crucial to addressing fluid resuscitation goals in these patients, observational studies provide some insight on the impact of the guidelines in practice. Due to inconsistent and insufficient evidence, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis within the available literature to shed some light on this dilemma.

We examined whether a restricted fluid resuscitation strategy of &lt;30 mL/kg IV crystalloids within the first three hours (&lt;30 × 3) affects in-hospital mortality compared to the SSCB recommendation of at least 30 mL/kg by 3 h (30 × 3) in sepsis patients with a history of HF.

## 1. Introduction

Sepsis is associated with significant burden and cost to the health care system in the United States. A study among Medicare beneficiaries from 2012 to 2018 showed an increase in hospital admissions for sepsis from 811,644 to 1,136,889, with total cost increase from $17,792,657,303 to $22,439,794,212 [1]. In the same study, 6-month mortality was 60% for septic shock, 36% for severe sepsis, 31% for sepsis attributed to a specific organism, and 27% for unspecified sepsis.

## 2. Materials and methods

## 2.1. Hypothesis

Wehypothesized that a restricted fluid resuscitation strategy using &lt;30 × 3 IV crystalloids, would not significantly affect in-hospital mortality, compared to adherence to the 30 × 3 SSCB recommendation in patients with a history of HF.

## 2.2. Method of search

A systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted according to PRISMA guidelines. A search in Embase, PubMed, and Scopus databases wasperformed on 03/07/2023. The following search syntax was used to search peer-reviewed papers, abstracts, and RCTs, based on their title, abstract, and keywords: ' heart failure ' or ' congestive heart failure ' or ' heart decompensation ' or ' cardiac failure ' and ' sepsis ' or ' septic shock ' and 'fl uid resuscitation ' or ' crystalloid ' or ' 30 by 3 ' , and ' mortality ' or ' in-hospital mortality ' (Supplemental table 1).

## 2.3. Selection criteria

The language was limited to English. One author (AW.) reviewed the search result titles and abstract, and two authors (AW., AV.) independently reviewed the eligible records. Disagreements over the selection of relevant papers were resolved through discussion and consensus. Studies published since 2016 included if they had sepsis patients with a history of HF (or a subgroup of patients with HF) and in-hospital mortality data on the patients that did or did not meet the 30 × 3 goal. Duplicated records, studies with unrelated title/abstract, studies that focused on a period broader than 3 h from the diagnosis of sepsis, and studies without mortality breakdown for HF patients or without IRB approval were excluded.

## 2.4. Search result

The search yielded 26,069 records, which were reduced to 12,549 after removing duplicates. After removing records with irrelevant titles or abstracts, this decreased to 9 (Fig. 1). Of these, 2 were excluded because no clear timeframe for fluid administration was provided, and 3 others were excluded because no raw mortality data was provided. After assessment for risk of bias by three authors, 4 final studies were considered for analysis (Table 1).

## 2.5. Assessing risk of bias

The Newcastle-Ottawa Scale for case-control studies and retrospective cohorts was used by three authors (AW., AV., AC.) to assess the risk of bias in the included studies (Supplemental Table 2,3,4). The included studies received a rating of &gt;6 stars by each author.

## 2.6. Statistical analyses

The meta-analysis was performed using the Review Manager 5.4 program (The Cochrane Collaboration, 2020) [9] by the random effects model with OR as the effect measure. The OR and 95% CI for mortality among HF patients with sepsis who received &lt;30 × 3 vs. 30 × 3 were calculated for each study. The mortality data were totaled, and the studies were weighted to calculate a total OR and 95% CI (Fig. 2). The program calculated the I 2 statistic for heterogeneity analysis. I 2 estimates the proportion of variance that is due to heterogeneity. This metric denotes the proportion of variation throughout the included studies that is due to heterogeneity rather than chance [10,11]. The ProMeta program version 3.0 [12] was used to evaluate publication bias. This program generated a funnel plot (Fig. 3), and determined the degree of asymmetry using Egger's linear regression test and Begg and Mazumdar's rank correlation test (Fig. 4). [13] [14].

Using Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services data, Rai et al. [18] , analyzed the association between receiving greater than versus &lt;30 mL/kg fluid and in-hospital mortality (adjusting for age, sex, race, and Charlson score) in a subset of patients with a history of HF and sepsis. They suggested that a larger cohort is required to support the benefit of 30 × 3 recommendation and its safety in patients with HF.

## 3.2. Statistical analysis results

When the data from the included studies were pooled, 571 patients with sepsis and a history of HF were identified. In the &lt;30 × 3 group, 74 of the 261 individuals died, and the number of deaths in the 30 × 3

group was 82 of the 310 individuals (Table 2). The weighted OR for death was significantly higher in the &lt;30 × 3 vs. 30 × 3 (OR = 1.81, 95% CI = 1.13 - 2.89, p =0.01). Heterogeneity analysis resulted in an I 2 of 0% (Fig. 2), demonstrating a high degree of homogeneity among the results of the selected studies and pooled data.

## 3.3. Risk of bias

The Newcastle-Ottawa Scale for case-control studies and retrospective cohorts was used by three authors (AW., AV., AC.) to assess the risk of bias in the included studies, all of which received a rating of &gt;6 stars by each author (Supplemental Table 2,3,4).

Publication bias analysis was performed by Egger's linear regression and Begg and Mazumdar's rank correlation test and displayed by funnel plot (Fig. 3). Egger's test revealed a p value of 0.95; and Begg and Mazumdar's rank test revealed a p value of 1.0, both suggesting no significant publication bias. This is visually demonstrated by a symmetric funnel plot.

## 4. Discussion

Our meta-analysis finds a significantly increased risk of inhospital mortality among hospitalized HF patients with sepsis/septic shock who received a restricted resuscitation strategy compared to those who met the 30 × 3 goal. This finding is consistent with that of Taenzer et al. and is aligned with the findings of other included research that, while not statistically significant, pointed in the same direction (Fig. 2).

Their data were excluded from our metaanalysis because they focused on bolus delivery within the first 6 h following sepsis diagnosis instead of 3.

Other studies like Wiczorek et al. [19] found no statistically significant association between resuscitation with &gt;30 × 3 in their at-risk group (HF and/or chronic kidney disease patients) with severe sepsis/septic shock and in-hospital mortality by 3 or 6 h. Likewise, Chiang et al. [20] found no significant difference in mortality in patients with HF or ESRD and sepsis/septic shock who received &lt;30 mL/kg by 3 h. However, the &gt;30 × 3 group had lower mortality at 6 h. The breakdown of mortality data in these two studies for the 30 × 3 group was not provided, so we were unable to incorporate them into our meta-analysis. Acharya et al. The method we used was based on a validated systematic review method and clinical factors. We used three different databases to capture a large potential pool of sources using the same search terms. However, only 4 out of 12,549 unique records with mortality data for HF patients who did or did not meet the 30 × 3 fluid resuscitation recommendation were identified. This database search result highlighted the scarcity of comparable studies on this topic. All 4 included studies discussing the administration of 30 × 3 to HF patients with sepsis were retrospective and observational, therefore a risk of confounding and data limitation must be considered. Selection bias, publication bias and other confounders are inevitable in this type of research. We sought to overcome these and other methodological concerns by following the PRISMA guidelines, carefully and restrictively specifying the inclusion/exclusion criteria, and employing a clear definition of the exposure and outcome. Using these measures in this study, however, influences the number of selected studies and the sample size, thereby reducing its statistical power. In conducting our analysis, we used several methods to reduce potential bias due to study selection. Our formal literature search was performed without limiting the search by the outcome. All studies included were retrospective or observational, obviating the need to search prospective trial registers. The studies were reviewed by three authors independently. Publication bias analysis was performed by Egger's linear regression; and Begg and Mazumdar's rank correlation tests; and displayed by funnel plot, demonstrating low likelihood of publication bias. However, the point estimate I 2 should be interpreted with caution when there are few studies in a meta-analysis [22].

## 5. Conclusion

There is scant evidence to guide acute resuscitation of high-risk septic patients, such as those with a history of HF, and providers' adherence to sepsis bundles in HF patients remains variable. Earlier trials (Rivers, ProCESS, ARISE, and ProMISe) analyzed IV fluid as part of a comprehensive sepsis protocol. Therefore, assumptions based solely on IV fluid approaches in high-risk patients are limited. Confounders, on the other hand, would impact observational research based on retrospective data. There is a clinical debate about the optimal IV fluid resuscitation strategy. We merely evaluated the existing evidence by a metaanalysis approach. Based on the published research, our meta-analysis revealed that a restricted fluid resuscitation strategy (&lt;30 mL/kg fluids within 3 h) in sepsis/septic shock patients with a history of HF was associated with increased risk of in-hospital mortality, while the included primary studies didn't achieve a statistical significance, except for the Taenzer et al. study.

Our study highlights the need for extensive research to properly address the fluid resuscitation approach in this group of patients. Additional research in larger and more diverse populations may also consider the impact of HF with reduced ejection fraction and preserved ejection fraction phenotypes, the impact of pressors, the severity of sepsis, non-ICU sepsis admissions, and use of invasive hemodynamic monitoring to guide resuscitation.
