## Abstract

Background: Opioids are often prescribed for acute pain to patients discharged from the emergency department (ED), but there is a paucity of data on their short-­ term use. The purpose of this study was to synthesize the evidence regarding the efficacy of prescribed opioids compared to nonopioid analgesics for acute pain relief in ED-­ discharged patients.

Methods: MEDLINE, EMBASE, CINAHL, PsycINFO, CENTRAL, and gray literature databases were searched from inception to January 2023. Two independent reviewers selected randomized controlled trials investigating the efficacy of prescribed opioids for ED-­ discharged patients, extracted data, and assessed risk of bias. Authors were contacted for missing data and to identify additional studies. The primary outcome was the difference in pain intensity scores or pain relief. All meta-­ analyses used a random-­ effect model and a sensitivity analysis compared patients treated with codeine versus those treated with other opioids.

## I NTRODUCTION

In  2019-­ 2020, 8.1% of all emergency department (ED) visits in the United States resulted in an opioid prescription at discharge. 1 Though a decrease in opioid prescribing has been observed in recent years, 2-­ 4 ED  physicians  still  prescribed  opioids  to  treat  moderate  to  severe acute pain at home. 5-­ 8 These prescription opioids are associated with adverse  events  (e.g.,  respiratory  depression,  constipation,  nausea/ vomiting,  dry  mouth,  dizziness,  drowsiness,  weakness,  pruritus) 9-­ 11 and can lead to long-­ term use, 12,13 opioid misuse, and substance use disorder 14 or death. 15 However, alternatives such as nonsteroidal anti-­ inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) are also associated with adverse events (e.g.,  gastrointestinal  bleeding,  acute  stroke,  myocardial  infarction, congestive  heart  failure,  cardiovascular  death,  hypertension,  acute renal failure), particularly in older patients. 16,17

The efficacy of opioids for outpatient acute pain management has been well studied in the postoperative context. A systematic review on postoperative pain showed no pain intensity differences within a 24-­ hour follow-­ up period between opioids and NSAIDs and also between opioids and acetaminophen. 18 A systematic review comparing codeine to NSAIDs in postoperative pain showed better pain scores and fewer adverse events in adult outpatients treated with NSAIDs. 19 In  a  recent  postsurgical  randomized  controlled  dental  pain  study, naproxen (NSAID) was significantly  more  effective  at  reducing  pain intensity and relief over 12 h than a combination of hydrocodone and acetaminophen. 20 Furthermore, an overview of systematic reviews on the management of mainly postoperative acute dental pain showed that NSAIDs were the best option. 21 Postoperative opioids, particularly codeine or in the context of dental pain, do not seem to be superior to nonopioid analgesics.

Results: From 5419 initially screened citations,  46  full  texts were  evaluated  and  six studies enrolling 1161 patients were included. Risk of bias was low for five studies. There was no statistically significant difference in pain intensity scores or pain relief between opioids versus nonopioid analgesics (standardized mean difference [SMD] 0.12; 95% confidence interval [CI] -0.10 to 0.34). Contrary to children, adult patients treated with opioid had better pain relief (SMD 0.28, 95% CI 0.13-­ 0.42) compared to nonopioids. In another sensitivity analysis excluding studies using codeine, opioids were more effective than nonopioids (SMD 0.30, 95% CI 0.15-­ 0.45). However, there were more adverse events associated with opioids (odds ratio 2.64, 95% CI 2.04-­ 3.42).

Conclusions: For ED-­ discharged patients with acute musculoskeletal pain, opioids do not seem to be more effective than nonopioid analgesics. However, this absence of efficacy seems to be driven by codeine, as opioids other than codeine are more effective than nonopioids (mostly NSAIDs). Further prospective studies on the efficacy of short-­ term opioid use after ED discharge (excluding codeine), measuring patient-­ centered outcomes, adverse events, and potential misuse, are needed.

## KEYWORDS

emergency department, opioids, outpatients, pain

The efficacy of opioids administered during an ED visit has also been well studied. For acute musculoskeletal injuries seen in the ED, opioids do not seem to offer a better benefit-­ harm ratio in relieving pain than NSAIDs. 22-­ 24 Others have found that opioids are not superior to NSAIDs or acetaminophen at reducing acute pain in a prehospital setting. 25 However, another literature review on musculoskeletal pain in the ED reported that 'No interventions were more effective at decreasing pain intensity than opioids at 60 min.' 26 Furthermore, a prospective cohort study of ED patients discharged with an opioid prescription showed that patients using opioids were more likely to report pain relief than those using NSAIDs and/or acetaminophen. 27 Nonetheless, the study was observational, not controlled or randomized. Even so most of these studies do not address pain management after ED discharge. Analgesics administered only in the ED could provide a different effect at home after the initial ED treatment of acute pain. 28 Given  that  opioids  are  prescribed  to  ED-­ discharged  patients for acute pain and the conflicting evidence regarding their short-­ term use, we aimed to synthesize the evidence regarding the efficacy of prescribed opioids compared to nonopioid analgesics for acute pain relief in ED-­ discharged patients.

## METHODS

## Review design

This  systematic  review  and  meta-­ analysis  was  prospectively  registered  (PROSPERO  CRD42022332009).  Its  results  are  presented as  per  the  Preferred  Reporting  Items  for  Systematic  Reviews  and Meta-­ Analysis  (PRISMA)  guidelines  (Data  S1). 29 We  also  followed et al

.

the synthesis without meta-­ analysis reporting guidelines to complement the PRISMA guidelines. 30

## Eligibility criteria

We included  randomized  controlled  trials  (RCT)  and  cluster  RCTs designed to evaluate the efficacy of opioids prescribed for relief of acute pain in ED-­ discharged patients compared to nonopioid analgesics. All types of pain complaints were considered.

We excluded studies exclusively on patients with substance use disorder or chronic pain, evaluating only opioids given during the ED stay, comparing different opioids head-­ to-­ head, not reporting pain intensities or pain relief (after contacting authors), or available only as abstracts. There were no exclusions based on language or date of publication.

## Data sources and search strategy

In collaboration with an information specialist, we developed a search strategy based on four search themes: 'opioid,' 'pain,' 'emergency,' and 'effectiveness' (detailed search strategies are available in Supplement Methods S1 ). The following databases were searched from their inception to May 5, 2022: CENTRAL, MEDLINE, EMBASE, CINAHL, and PsycINFO. An updated search was performed at the end of the process during the third week of January 2023 to collect recently published papers. We also searched Clini calTr ials.gov and the International Standard  Randomized  Controlled  Trial  Number  registry  for  ongoing studies.  Gray  literature was  searched  using  Google  Scholar  and  the first  300  hits were screened to identify any relevant studies. References from studies meeting inclusion criteria were examined and their authors were contacted to identify additional relevant studies.

## Study selection

Identified references from all databases were transferred to Covidence systematic  review  manager  (Covidence  systematic  review  software, Veritas Health Innovation, available at www.covid ence.org). Eight reviewers (RD, JP, MM, DW, GF, AF, AO, and AC) participated in a two-­ stage  selection  process  of  eligible  studies.  After  duplicate  removal, each citation was screened by two independent reviewers using the title and abstract for potential relevance. The full text of the remaining citations was then independently evaluated by two reviewers against inclusion and exclusion criteria. During both stages, a third reviewer (RD or AC) resolved discrepancies.

## Data extraction

Data  were  extracted  independently  by  two  reviewers  (JP  and MM) using a standardized electronic sheet, and the conflicts were

|

resolved  by  consensus.  Study  authors  were  contacted  when  outcomes were incomplete or when ED patients were mixed in with other populations. Sample size, age range, inclusion/exclusion criteria, type of opioid, type of nonopioid analgesic, outcomes, and primary and secondary results were extracted.

## Primary and secondary outcomes

Our primary outcome was the difference in pain intensity scores or pain relief between prescribed opioids and nonopioid analgesics at any time after an ED visit for acute pain. Our secondary outcomes were the percentage of patients with adverse events and the percentage of patients requiring rescue analgesics.

## Risk-­ of-­ bias assessment

Risk of bias was assessed using the Effective Practice and Organization  of  Care  (EPOC)  risk-­ of-­ bias  tool. 31 Two  reviewers  (JP  and MM) assessed the risk of bias independently, with conflicts resolved through  discussion  or  involvement  of  a  third  reviewer  (RD).  RCTs were ranked as having low risk, being unclear, or having high risk of bias across nine domains (i.e., random sequence generation, allocation concealment, similarity of baseline outcome measurements, similarity  of  baseline  characteristics,  incomplete  outcome  data, knowledge of the allocated interventions adequately prevented during the study, protection against contamination, selective outcome reporting, and other risk of bias) and the overall risk of bias was derived. Risk-­ of-­ bias judgments for each domain across studies were summarized using risk-­ of-­ bias graphs and summaries. Unpublished studies were considered at high risk of bias.

## Data synthesis and analysis

The difference in pain intensity scores (or in pain relief) between patients who were administered opioids versus those not administered opioids from baseline to cutoff time as specified in the original RCT (the final single time point from each follow-­ up were used for the main analysis), adverse events incidence, utilization of rescue analgesics, and data related to patient demographics were reported for each study.

If data were available for at least three studies, results for the main outcome and for adverse events were pooled and meta-­ analyzed. For the main efficacy outcome, studies that reported mean and standard deviation  (SD)  of  pain  relief  for  both  opioid  and  nonopioid  groups were expressed by standard mean differences (SMD) in forest plots. For studies that presented the percentage of patients with pain relief  or  with  treatment  failure,  data  were  converted  to  SMD  using  a meta-­ analysis  software  (Comprehensive  Meta-­ analysis:  A  Computer Program for Research Synthesis, Biostat, 2005). 32 Data extracted for adverse events were all reported with the percentage of patients with

|

any side effects and were expressed in odds ratios (ORs). Sensitivity analyses  were  performed  for  studies  with  available  short-­ term  follow-­ up (≤3 days) to examine whether opioids were more effective in this situation. Another sensitivity analysis compared patients treated with codeine versus those treated with other opioids since codeine is a prodrug and its analgesic properties depend on its conversion to morphine, which varies considerably between subjects. 33 Sensitivity analyses were also performed excluding studies with high risk of bias and for studies involving a pediatric population compared to adults. Finally, we repeated the same analyses while excluding studies for which the comparator was only a placebo.

Heterogeneity was statistically assessed both overall and for each sensitivity analysis using I 2 , tau-­ square, and Cochran's Q tests. Prediction intervals were not presented since they can be problematic when the number of studies is small ( n &lt; 10). 34 A chi-­ square test was used to determine whether there was a difference between sensitivity categories.  All  effect  sizes  are  reported  with  95%  confidence  intervals (CIs). All analyses were conducted using an inverse variance weighting method and a random-­ effect model, even if the I 2 was low, due to the clinical heterogeneity of painful conditions, outcomes, and treatments. 35 Forest plots were performed using Revman, version 5.4 (The Nordic Cochrane Centre, The Cochrane Collaboration, 2014). No tests for publication bias were done (funnel plot asymmetry) because the power of the test is too low to distinguish chance from real asymmetry when there are fewer than 10 studies. 34

## RESULTS

## Search results and study characteristics

The initial search strategy generated 5942 references after duplicate removal. Of these, 47 were kept for full-­ text review and seven studies were retained. One of them 36 was further removed since it used dextropropoxyphene, an opioid that has been withdrawn from the market in  2009 because of its association with lethal events. Therefore, six studies 37-­ 42 were finally included in this systematic review and meta-­ analysis (Figure 1 ). One study 39 compared two types of opioids (oxycodone and tramadol) to a placebo. Patients could consume rescue medications and placebo has demonstrated analgesic effects 43,44 ; we kept only the oxycodone versus placebo comparison as tramadol has nonopioid analgesic effects. 45 Another study 42 compared oxycodone to two nonopioids (NSAID and cyclobenzaprine); we kept only the oxycodone versus NSAIDs comparison since cyclobenzaprine is mainly a muscle relaxant. 46 One conflict on data extraction for the outcome measured in the study by Drendel et al. 40 was settled through consensus. Four differences in risk of bias assessment were observed between the two raters, two in the protection against contamination and two in the selective outcome reporting domain. These were resolved through consensus by the two raters.

Four  included  studies 37,39,40,42 were  conducted  in  the  United States and two 38,41 in  Canada;  four  studies 37-­ 39,42 included adult and  two  others 40,41 included  pediatric  populations  (Table 1 ). All  studies  included  patients  with  musculoskeletal  pain;  five  of them 37,38,40-­ 42 had  NSAIDs  as  a  comparator  and  one  had  a  placebo. 39 Three studies 37,38,40 used  codeine as the opioid, and the three  others  used  hydrocodone, 39 morphine, 41 or  oxycodone. 42 Except for morphine, opioids were combined with acetaminophen and their dosage and administration frequency were not equivalent. The type of NSAIDs used also varied across studies: diflunisal, ketorolac, ibuprofen (two studies), and naproxen. Follow-­ up duration varied from 1 to 7 days and outcomes were pain relief for five studies 37-­ 39,41,42 and treatment failure (use of rescue medication) for the other. 40 The majority of studies applied extensive exclusion criteria (Supplement Table S2 ).

## Meta-­ analysis

The data for opioid efficacy and side effects analysis from the six studies were available for meta-­ analysis. The number of included patients was 584 for the opioid group and 577 for the nonopioid group. The forest plot showed that opioids were not more effective than et al

.

nonopioids in the treatment of acute musculoskeletal pain after ED discharge (SMD 0.12, 95% CI -0.10 to 0.34, I 2 = 62%; Figure 2 ). Furthermore, patients treated with opioids experienced a higher rate of side effects than those treated with NSAIDs or placebo (OR 2.64, 95% CI 2.04-­ 3.42, I 2 = 0%; Figure 3 ).

## Sensitivity analyses

The results of opioid efficacy for studies with data on a short-­ term follow-­ up (≤3 days) 38-­ 41 or  without the study at high risk of bias 37 were similar as those reported from the main analysis (SMD 0.12, 95% CI -0.20 to 0.45, I 2 = 77%; and SMD 0.12, 95% CI -0.12 to 0.37, I 2 = 69%; Supplement Figures S2 and S3 ). Adult patients treated with opioids had better pain relief than those treated with nonopioids (SMD 0.28, 95% CI 0.13-­ 0.42, I 2 = 0%; Supplement Figure S4 ) contrary to children who had nonsignificant difference between opioids and nonopioids ( p = 0.78). However, the comparison of studies with pediatric  versus  adult  patients  showed  no  statistically  significant difference  between  these  subgroups  ( χ 2 = 1.78, p = 0.18).  When we removed the study 39 that had a placebo as a comparator in the adult subgroup, the effect was similar but was no longer statistically significant  (SMD 0.16,  95%  CI  -0.06  to  0.38, I 2 = 0%;  Supplement Figure S5 ).

The comparison of studies using codeine versus opioids other than  codeine  showed  a  significant  difference  between  these subgroups ( χ 2 = 7.47, p = 0.006).  Codeine was not more effective than  nonopioid  medications  (SMD -0.12,  95%  CI  -0.38  to  0.14,

## DISCUSSION

## Main finding summary

This  meta-­ analysis  of  studies  originating  from  the  United  States and Canada on adult and pediatric populations showed that opioids are not more effective than nonopioids (mostly NSAIDs) in managing  acute  pain  after  ED  discharge.  However,  a  sensitivity  analysis showed that opioids other than codeine provided better pain relief than nonopioids. Additionally, opioids were associated in all studies with a higher rate of adverse events and in one study with higher use of rescue medications. 41 While we planned to include RCTs on all types of acute outpatient pain management after ED discharge, we found only studies including patients with musculoskeletal injuries. We did not observe a significant difference in results between adult and pediatric populations.

## Risk of bias

Five studies 38-­ 42 were at low risk of bias and only one study 37 on adults with an ankle sprain was at high risk of bias because of the lack of intervention allocation concealment and different baseline characteristics. Nevertheless, after removing this study in a sensitivity analysis, results were similar which demonstrates their consistency. There was significant heterogeneity in results from studies on opioid efficacy, but this was mostly due to the poor analgesic effect of codeine. Heterogeneity was very low for studies using opioids other than codeine, as it was for adverse events associated with opioids in general. Furthermore, one study 42 on acute low-­ back pain found that adding oxycodone/acetaminophen to naproxen alone did not improve functional outcomes or pain at 1-­ week follow-­ up. In this study the oxycodone/acetaminophen was prescribed every 8 h as needed; since the elimination half-­ life of oxycodone is 3.51 h (SD ± 1.43) 47 and that oxycodone is typically given every 4 to 6 h, 48 this could explain the ineffectiveness of oxycodone.

## Comparison with previous systematic reviews

A 2022 systematic review examined the impact of opioids on adults during and after their ED stay. 49 They concluded that, 'Opioids may have pain outcomes equivalent to those of NSAIDs, but evidence on comparisons of harms is very uncertain and heterogeneous.' However, they did not perform a meta-­ analysis. Nevertheless, they only included two  studies  on  opioids  prescribed  upon  ED  discharge,  which  were included in our study. 37,42 We identified two other adult studies 38,39 with data on opioids after discharge from the ED that they included only in the 'opioids prescribed in the ED' section. A systematic review published in 2020 evaluated outpatient acute pain management (medication and other approaches) from non-­ low-­ back musculoskeletal injuries in adults and found that opioids combined with acetaminophen compared to placebo improved pain from Day 1 to 7, but not for immediate pain (less than 2 h), and caused more adverse events. 22 However, they included all parallel-­ design studies on musculoskeletal injuries in any outpatient setting and studies with a single dose of opioids; results could  be  different  for  acute  pain  management  in  an  ED-­ discharged population.  The  efficacy  of  treatment  (medication  and  other  approaches) for acute and subacute nonspecific low-­ back pain has been examined in another systematic review. 50 They concluded that to reduce pain intensity, the most efficacious interventions (up to 1 week) were heat wrap, manual therapy, exercise, NSAIDs, and opioids; mild or moderate adverse events were most often associated with opioids, NSAIDs, and steroids. However, they identified only three studies on opioids; two were performed in the ED and one was conducted in an outpatient  setting  using  meptazinol, 51 which  is  seldomly  used  and available only in a few European countries.

Systematic reviews on specific painful conditions obtained results aligned with ours. For osteoarthritis pain, one review concluded with overall low confidence that the effect of NSAIDs for knee pain was similar to that of opioids. 52 However, another systematic review on osteoarthritis not limited to the knee found that opioids may provide a small benefit in pain and disability but may also increase the risk of adverse events. As they did not encounter a study using codeine, their results are comparable to our sensitivity analysis excluding codeine. 53 Moreover, a systematic review on postoperative pain demonstrated that codeine provides less pain relief and more adverse events than NSAIDs. 19 Another study, evaluating codeine/acetaminophen and oxycodone/acetaminophen combinations on the first day after ED discharge, demonstrated a not statistically significant trend in more pain relief with oxycodone/acetaminophen (66% vs. 61%). 54 However, one review examined the outpatient treatment of acute pain in general (surgical and nonsurgical) and concluded that opioid therapy was associated with similar efficacy as an NSAID and increased risk of short-­ term adverse events. 18

Many recent studies showed that NSAIDs or acetaminophen are as good as or better than opioids for the treatment of acute pain. 18-­ 25

Due to the opioid crisis and the postulate that short-­ term opioids are ineffective and associated with a high risk of abuse, some health care professionals have stopped using opioids in the ED. 55 Most opioid prescribing guidelines for chronic noncancer pain do not adequately guide ED acute care. 56 The American College of Emergency Physicians 2020 clinical policy on adult opioid prescribing presented Level C recommendations to preferentially prescribe nonopioid analgesics for acute painful conditions of ED-­ discharged patients. 57 However, they also state that excessive limitation of opioid use in that context  may  lead  to  inadequate  pain  management.  A  position  statement by the American Academy of Emergency Medicine suggests using a short-­ course (2-­ 3 days) regimen of opioids, while excluding some medical conditions (e.g.,  chronic  obstructive  pulmonary  disease,  sleep  apnea,  substance  use  disorder)  and  being  mindful  of total  acetaminophen  exposures  with  combination  opioids. 58 The Canadian Association of Emergency Medicine provides a guideline for ED management of opioid use disorder, but they do not provide guidance on ED opioid prescribing. 59 In our review, codeine did not provide better pain relief than nonopioids. However, codeine combined with acetaminophen is often selected has a low-­ potency opioid even if some clinicians propose to completely stop using it. 60,61 Furthermore, a prospective cohort study of ED patients discharged with an opioid prescription, which excluded codeine or combination with acetaminophen, showed that patients using opioids were more likely  to  report  pain  relief  than  those  using  NSAIDs and/or acetaminophen, even if they reported more severe baseline pain intensity. 27 This approach allows optimal dosage of both acetaminophen and the opioid and in the same cohort less than 1% of patients misused opioids at 3 months. 62 Yet, the risk of long-­ term use or misuse after a short course of opioids for acute pain remains poorly studied. Additionally, not using or using low-­ potency opioids for acute post­ ED pain may influence the development of chronic pain, as some pain trajectories in the 2 weeks after an ED visit are associated with chronic pain. 63

There is a need to develop quality research and effective strategies to optimize, but not necessarily outright eliminate, opioid prescribing upon ED discharge. For example, it might be beneficial for selected patients to be prescribed a small quantity of a specific type of  opioid  (not  combination  with  acetaminophen),  which  could  be served in even smaller portions by the pharmacist and associated with  optimal  dosage  of  acetaminophen  and/or  NSAIDs. 64,65 Such strategies will require prospective research to further delineate appropriate indications and optimize risk stratification, type of opioid, and  prescription  method/duration.  These  studies  should  measure patient-­ centered  outcomes  and  ensure  equitable  treatment  for  all ED-­ discharged patients.

## LIMITATIONS

Our  review  is  limited  by  the  study  population's  heterogeneity (pediatric  and  adult),  medications  type/dosage,  and  follow-­ up duration.  However,  analyses  were  done  using  a  random-­ effect

|

model,  which  partially  compensated  for  this  diversity  and  after excluding codeine, the heterogeneity was very low ( I 2 = 0%). Furthermore, results were consistent in sensitivity analyses on the adult population and in which the study with high risk of bias and the one with only placebo as a comparison was excluded. Also, we studied only opioid use after ED discharge; results could be different in another context. Most studies utilized extensive exclusion  criteria  and  included  only  patients  with  musculoskeletal injuries in a North American population, which limit their external validity. The adult studies included only painful syndromes of sprains and strains, and none had fractures that might have been better relieved by opioids. Most included studies (five of six) used a combination of acetaminophen (one also added an NSAID) and opioid  (two  of  them  at  higher  acetaminophen  doses  than  what is  currently  permitted)  instead  of  opioid  alone,  which  may  have contributed to opioid efficacy. Finally, included studies were not designed to assess the risk of overdose, opioid use disorder, or long-­ term harms.

## CONCLUSIONS

In summary, this meta-­ analysis demonstrated that for ED-­ discharged patients  with  acute  musculoskeletal  pain,  opioids  do not  seem  more  effective  than  nonopioid  analgesics.  However, this absence of efficacy seems to be driven by codeine, as other opioids are more effective than nonopioids (mostly nonsteroidal anti-­ inflammatory  drugs)  but  also  associated  with  more  adverse events. Further prospective studies on the efficacy of short-­ term opioid  use  after  ED  discharge  (excluding  codeine),  measuring patient-­ centered outcomes, adverse events, and potential misuse, are needed.
